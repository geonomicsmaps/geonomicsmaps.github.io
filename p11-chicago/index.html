How Chicago Lifted Its Buildings With People Still Inside

The hotel that went up

One Monday morning in Chicago, a six-story brick hotel started rising off the ground.

  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Feb. 26, 1861, p. 1, 'One, Two, Three, and Up She Goes!', 1861
    Five thousand screws, each ten of which has the entire attention of one man

It was open for business, and its guests were still inside.

  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Jan. 22 and 24, 1861, p. 1, 1861
    it is proposed to keep the House open, and its business as usual
  • Chicago Daily Tribune, July 26, 1861, p. 4, 'The Reopening of the Tremont House', 1861 partly confirmed
    only the most consummate tact in hotel management could have kept the Tremont open, its business only moderately diminished

By evening, the whole building stood a full foot higher.

  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Feb. 26, 1861, p. 1, 'One, Two, Three, and Up She Goes!', 1861
    Five thousand screws, each ten of which has the entire attention of one man

So how did Chicago lift its buildings with people still inside? And why would anyone try? The hotel wasn't the first, and it wasn't the last. For years, Chicago jacked up brick buildings like this, with business going on inside: stores, a bank, a whole block of shops.

  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Jan. 26, 1858, p. 1, 'The House Raising on Randolph Street', 1858
    some two hundred screws and fifteen men being employed in the operation
  • Chicago Press and Tribune, Apr. 2, 1860 (reprinted in the Janesville Daily Gazette, Apr. 3, 1860, and The Builder, London, May 19, 1860), 1860
    filled with occupants ... the ceaseless daily tide of pedestrians has not been impeded
  • Chicago Tribune, Nov. 10, 1866, p. 2, 'Building in Chicago', 1866 partly confirmed
    raising the Briggs House ... while the business of the hotel was going on without interruption, cost with attendant improvements, $35,000
  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 2 (1885), St. Patrick's Church, 1871
    At the time of the fire the building was standing on the screws used to elevate it to the grade

The reason was buried under the street. Or rather, it couldn't be buried. The city sat too low to drain. Its new sewers couldn't go down. So the streets had to come up, and many buildings went up with them.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), quoting assistant engineer Clarke, 1855
    the surface of the ground along the North and South branches of the river was only three or four feet above the average surface of the lake
  • Report and Plan of Sewerage for the City of Chicago, Board of Sewerage Commissioners, 31.12.1855, 1855
    the necessity of keeping the top of the sewers as low as possible, to avoid expense in raising the grades of the streets
  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), assistant engineer Clarke, 1855
    it was decided to fill to a level of ten feet above ordinary water on the streets adjacent to the river
  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Apr. 9, 1857, p. 1, 'A Higher Grade', 1857
    every house now built must be raised about the hight of the Mayor above its present foundation, or be entered through doors cut in its second story

We'll get to how they did it, and who paid. And why, a decade later, that building was gone.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 2 (1885), account of the fire, 1871
    the Briggs, Sherman, Tremont and other large hotels, Wood's Museum, the beautiful structures of Lake and Randolph streets

Why was Chicago stuck in the mud?

In the 1850s, many of Chicago's streets were paved with wooden planks. Come spring, you'd see the planks actually float.

  • Putnam's Monthly, June 1856, p. 610 ('Chicago in 1856'), 1856 partly confirmed
    The planks actually float, and, as the heavy wagons pass along, ornamental jets of muddy water play on the every side.

When a heavy wagon rolled past, jets of muddy water shot up on every side.

  • Putnam's Monthly, June 1856, p. 610 ('Chicago in 1856'), 1856 partly confirmed
    The planks actually float, and, as the heavy wagons pass along, ornamental jets of muddy water play on the every side.

Here's why. Along the river, the ground was only three or four feet above the lake.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), quoting assistant engineer Clarke, 1855
    the surface of the ground along the North and South branches of the river was only three or four feet above the average surface of the lake

Water needs a slope to run off. Chicago barely had one.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), quoting assistant engineer Clarke, 1855
    the surface of the ground along the North and South branches of the river was only three or four feet above the average surface of the lake

The city had already tried digging down. It cut Lake Street to the level of the lake and laid planks on top.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), quoting W. Bross, 1840
    the experiment was a disastrous failure, for the stench at once became intolerable

The stench became intolerable, and the street was filled back in.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), quoting W. Bross, 1840
    the experiment was a disastrous failure, for the stench at once became intolerable

Then came 1854. That year, cholera killed 1,424 people in Chicago.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), city mortality table; Encyclopedia of Chicago, 'Epidemics', 1854
    The deaths from the disease during the year were reported at 1,424.

That was about one in every forty-six residents.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), population and mortality tables (ratio: our calculation), 1854
    1854 ... 65,872 City

The next year, Chicago got a board of sewerage commissioners, with an engineer named Chesbrough in charge of the design.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), 1855
    insisted, from the first, upon the advantage of a high grade for the purpose of proper drainage and dry streets

The stated goal: the city's health.

  • Report and Plan of Sewerage for the City of Chicago (Chesbrough, 1855), p. 99, 1855
    As the main object of the sewers is to improve and preserve the health of the city

Why couldn't the sewers just go down?

The obvious fix is to bury sewers under the streets. That's what you'd expect. But a sewer runs on gravity. It has to slope downhill the whole way to the river.

  • Report and Plan of Sewerage for the City of Chicago, Board of Sewerage Commissioners, 31.12.1855, 1855
    the necessity of keeping the top of the sewers as low as possible, to avoid expense in raising the grades of the streets
  • Report and Plan of Sewerage for the City of Chicago (Chesbrough, 1855), p. 335, 1855
    dispense with pumping up the sewage

So the lowest point is fixed. Chesbrough put the outlets just above the lake's low-water line.

  • Report and Plan of Sewerage for the City of Chicago, Board of Sewerage Commissioners, 31.12.1855, 1855
    the necessity of keeping the top of the sewers as low as possible, to avoid expense in raising the grades of the streets

From there, every pipe had to climb as it ran back into the city. But near the river, the ground was only a few feet higher than the water. There was no room to bury them.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), quoting assistant engineer Clarke, 1855
    the surface of the ground along the North and South branches of the river was only three or four feet above the average surface of the lake
  • Report and Plan of Sewerage for the City of Chicago, Board of Sewerage Commissioners, 31.12.1855, 1855
    the necessity of keeping the top of the sewers as low as possible, to avoid expense in raising the grades of the streets
  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), assistant engineer Clarke, 1855
    it was decided to fill to a level of ten feet above ordinary water on the streets adjacent to the river

He kept the sewers as low as he could, to avoid the expense of raising the streets.

  • Report and Plan of Sewerage for the City of Chicago, Board of Sewerage Commissioners, 31.12.1855, 1855
    the necessity of keeping the top of the sewers as low as possible, to avoid expense in raising the grades of the streets

It wasn't enough. The sewers couldn't go down. So the streets had to come up.

  • Report and Plan of Sewerage for the City of Chicago, Board of Sewerage Commissioners, 31.12.1855, 1855
    the necessity of keeping the top of the sewers as low as possible, to avoid expense in raising the grades of the streets
  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), assistant engineer Clarke, 1855
    it was decided to fill to a level of ten feet above ordinary water on the streets adjacent to the river

The city set a new official street level, called the grade. Then, in 1857, it set a higher one.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), 1857
    May 4, the "high grade" was finally established on the South Side by a city ordinance.
  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Apr. 9, 1857, p. 1, 'A Higher Grade', 1857
    every house now built must be raised about the hight of the Mayor above its present foundation, or be entered through doors cut in its second story

Streets near the river would be filled in, several feet above the natural ground.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), assistant engineer Clarke, 1855
    it was decided to fill to a level of ten feet above ordinary water on the streets adjacent to the river
  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Apr. 9, 1857, p. 1, 'A Higher Grade', 1857
    every house now built must be raised about the hight of the Mayor above its present foundation, or be entered through doors cut in its second story

And where do you find that much dirt? Some of it came out of the river, dredged up and dumped on the courthouse square.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884): Van Osdel and Clarke, 1884
    the surplus earth of the South Division has been sufficient not only to raise the grade of the streets, but to fill up the whole of the lake …

"The height of the Mayor"

The buildings stayed where they were. The street didn't. Your front door was now in a pit. Your ground floor was turning into a basement. From one block to the next, the sidewalk could jump by up to five feet, with steps in between.

  • Putnam's Monthly, June 1856, p. 610 ('Chicago in 1856'), 1856 partly confirmed
    The planks actually float, and, as the heavy wagons pass along, ornamental jets of muddy water play on the every side.

In April 1857, the Chicago Tribune spelled out what the higher grade meant for every house already standing.

  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Apr. 9, 1857, p. 1, 'A Higher Grade', 1857
    every house now built must be raised about the hight of the Mayor above its present foundation, or be entered through doors cut in its second story

It "must be raised about the height of the Mayor above its present foundation, or be entered through doors cut in its second story."

  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Apr. 9, 1857, p. 1, 'A Higher Grade', 1857
    every house now built must be raised about the hight of the Mayor above its present foundation, or be entered through doors cut in its second story

The estimated damage to buildings already standing: two million dollars, at least.

  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Apr. 9, 1857, p. 1, 'A Higher Grade', 1857
    every house now built must be raised about the hight of the Mayor above its present foundation, or be entered through doors cut in its second story

In 2025 dollars, that's roughly seventy million.

Which raised the obvious question. Who must pay it?

  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Apr. 9, 1857, p. 1, 'A Higher Grade', 1857
    every house now built must be raised about the hight of the Mayor above its present foundation, or be entered through doors cut in its second story

Owners on Lake Street had already gone to court to stop the high grade. The judge turned them down.

  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 1 (1884), 1857

Who lifted the first brick building?

The first answer came from a drugstore. Thayer and Pike sat on the corner of Randolph and Dearborn, in a four-story brick block.

  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Jan. 26, 1858, p. 1, 'The House Raising on Randolph Street', 1858
    some two hundred screws and fifteen men being employed in the operation
  • Weekly Chicago Times, Jan. 21, 1858, p. 3, 'Bound to Rise', 1858 partly confirmed
    a large number of other establishments are doing business in the block

By one newspaper account, the idea was the druggist's. He went east and came back with a Boston contractor named James Brown.

  • Weekly Chicago Times, Jan. 21, 1858, p. 3, 'Bound to Rise', 1858 partly confirmed
    a large number of other establishments are doing business in the block

In January 1858, Brown set about two hundred screws under the walls.

  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Jan. 26, 1858, p. 1, 'The House Raising on Randolph Street', 1858
    some two hundred screws and fifteen men being employed in the operation

Fifteen men turned them. And the block went up a little over six feet.

  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Jan. 26, 1858, p. 1, 'The House Raising on Randolph Street', 1858
    some two hundred screws and fifteen men being employed in the operation

The drugstore stayed open while it happened. So did the other businesses in the block.

  • Weekly Chicago Times, Jan. 21, 1858, p. 3, 'Bound to Rise', 1858 partly confirmed
    a large number of other establishments are doing business in the block

The lift cost twenty-seven hundred dollars.

  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Jan. 26, 1858, p. 1, 'The House Raising on Randolph Street', 1858
    some two hundred screws and fifteen men being employed in the operation

And the owner got something back. The gap under the building became a brand-new basement.

Other owners were watching. By the end of that year, fifty to sixty brick stores had been raised.

The plan was to lift all of it together.

  • Janesville Daily Gazette, Mar. 26, 1860 (from the Chicago press), 1860
    There will be some 600 men employed, and about 6,000 screws.

Here's how a lift like this worked. Crews dug out the foundation and covered the trenches. Then they slid heavy timbers under the walls.

  • D. Macrae (1870), 1870
    at a given signal turn all the screws simultaneously

Under the timbers went the screws.

  • D. Macrae (1870), 1870
    at a given signal turn all the screws simultaneously

Think of the jack you'd use to change a tire. Now line up six thousand of them.

  • Chicago Press and Tribune, 2.4.1860 (Janesville 3.4.1860), 1860
    each man in charge of from eight to ten screws

Six hundred men worked the screws. Each man looked after eight to ten of them.

  • Chicago Press and Tribune, 2.4.1860 (Janesville 3.4.1860), 1860
    each man in charge of from eight to ten screws

Then a whistle blew. Every man gave each of his screws a quarter turn.

  • Robert Chambers, 'A City Elevated', Chambers’s Journal; The Living Age 30.3.1861, 1861
    the building has thus been raised a fourth part of that space throughout, or exactly 3-32d of an inch

And the whole block rose by about the thickness of three credit cards.

  • Robert Chambers, 'A City Elevated', Chambers’s Journal; The Living Age 30.3.1861, 1861
    the building has thus been raised a fourth part of that space throughout, or exactly 3-32d of an inch

Whistle, turn. Whistle, turn. About six hundred times.

  • Robert Chambers, 'A City Elevated', Chambers’s Journal; The Living Age 30.3.1861, 1861
    the building has thus been raised a fourth part of that space throughout, or exactly 3-32d of an inch

In five days, the block stood four feet eight inches higher.

  • Chicago Press and Tribune, 2.4.1860 (Janesville 3.4.1860); R. Chambers, Living Age 30.3.1861, 1860

What about the wooden houses?

Brick got lifted. A lot of wood got moved. Wooden houses were jacked up, set on rollers, and hauled down the street. A single horse walked in circles, winding in the rope.

  • D. Macrae (1870), 1870
    One day I met nine.

That same Scottish visitor once met nine houses on the move in a single day.

  • D. Macrae (1870), 1870
    One day I met nine.

People sat at the windows as they went. He saw a shopkeeper in his doorway, smoking a cigar, while the shop rolled along.

  • D. Macrae (1870), 1870
    One day I met nine.

Why move them at all? Downtown land was wanted for stone buildings, and rolling a house out to the suburbs was cheaper than tearing it down.

  • David Macrae, The Americans at Home (1870), 1870
    the ground is wanted for stone-buildings and warehouses; and it is found cheaper to move the wooden houses away to the suburbs

And the brick lifting kept going. In 1866, another six-story hotel, the Briggs House, went up about four feet.

Who paid?

And one line survived from Chesbrough's plan. He measured his sewers from the lake's low-water mark of 1847.

  • Report and Plan of Sewerage for the City of Chicago, Board of Sewerage Commissioners, 31.12.1855, 1855
    the necessity of keeping the top of the sewers as low as possible, to avoid expense in raising the grades of the streets

Chicago still measures its heights from a zero set at that same low water.

  • City of Chicago, Department of Water Management, 'Elevation Benchmarks' veri seti, 2011
    The benchmarks are referenced to the Chicago City Datum = 0.00, (CCD = 579.88 feet above mean tide New York).
  • Number One City Datum plaque, LaSalle and Monroe (Historical Marker Database), with Chicago Tribune, 1949 (interview with engineer Charles Jones), 1847
    It is from this point that all heights in the city are measured. / the datum was set at the low-water mark of Lake Michigan in Chicago circa …

So if you ever walk through downtown Chicago, look down. The height of that sidewalk is counted from the lake's low water of 1847.

Here's the scorecard. More than a decade of lifting, often four to six feet at a time.

  • Chicago Daily Tribune, Jan. 26, 1858, p. 1, 'The House Raising on Randolph Street', 1858
    some two hundred screws and fifteen men being employed in the operation
  • Chicago Daily Press and Tribune, Jan. 1, 1859, p. 3, 'City Improvements in 1858', 1858 partly confirmed
    Within the past year from fifty to sixty brick stores ... have been thus raised
  • A. T. Andreas, History of Chicago, vol. 2 (1885), St. Patrick's Church, 1871
    At the time of the fire the building was standing on the screws used to elevate it to the grade

The city paid for the pipes. The owners paid for the lifts. And the street level they paid for is still there.

  • Report of the Board of Sewerage Commissioners (second half of 1860), 1860
    The actual cost of the 53¾ miles of sewers, together with the catch-basins and man-holes built, is $686,277.80.
  • Chicago Press and Tribune, 2.4.1860 (Janesville 3.4.1860), 1860
    The contract was taken not jointly, but so carried out, by the several firms of Brown & Hollingsworth, Pullman & Moore, and Ely & Smith

Our calculations

  • Today's dollars: U.S. consumer price index (1967 = 100) from the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis: 1857 = 28, 1858 = 26, 1860 = 27, 2025 = 967.5 (values before 1913 are estimates). $2 million in 1857 ≈ $69 million; $2,700 in 1858 ≈ $100,000; $686,278 in 1860 ≈ $24.6 million; $17,000 in 1860 ≈ $610,000. Rounded; a price index is only a rough guide across 165 years.
  • "About one in every forty-six residents": 1,424 cholera deaths in 1854 against a city census population of 65,872 (Andreas 1884) = 1 in 46.
  • "The thickness of three credit cards": an eyewitness wrote that each quarter turn of the screws lifted the building 3/32 of an inch (about 2.4 mm). A standard card is 0.76 mm thick (ISO/IEC 7810), so three cards are about 2.3 mm.
  • "About six hundred times": 4 feet 8 inches is 56 inches; 56 ÷ 3/32 ≈ 597 quarter turns.
  • "More than twice that": new foundations and sidewalks cost an estimated $40,000 to $50,000 (eyewitness account, 1861), against $17,000 for the lift itself (Chicago Press and Tribune, 1860).

Worth knowing

  • Chicago did not raise the whole city at once. The new street grades of 1855-1857 were filled in street by street over about two decades, and owners decided building by building whether to lift, rebuild or live below the street.
  • George Pullman worked as one of several contractors (the Tremont House contract names "Ely, Smith & Pullman"). The first brick building in the city was lifted in January 1858 by James Brown of Boston, three years before the Tremont.
  • The Lake Street load of about 35,000 tons is a newspaper estimate from 1860, and "not a single pane of glass broke" is an eyewitness report; we say "estimated" and "reportedly".
  • "One newspaper account" for the drugstore's idea: the story that the druggist himself suggested the lift comes from a single 1858 report.
  • "About eighteen thousand buildings" and "roughly a hundred thousand people" for the 1871 fire are the Chicago Relief and Aid Society's figures, quoted by Andreas (1885).
  • Chicago City Datum: the city still measures elevations from a zero set at the low water of Lake Michigan in 1847, 579.88 feet above mean tide at New York (City of Chicago; benchmark plaque at LaSalle and Monroe).

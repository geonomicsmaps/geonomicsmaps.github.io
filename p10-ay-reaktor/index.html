Why can't solar do the job?

Here's the obvious question. Why not just use solar panels? On most of the Moon, a night lasts about two weeks.

  • NASA Glenn, SPARC Industry Day sunumu (25.9.2026 eki), 2026
    Lunar south pole 84°S to 90°S, on slopes up to 10 degrees, 354 hrs of darkness
  • U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Nuclear Energy, 2020
    enough to power four-hundred 100-watt light bulbs or roughly 1/25,000 of the power produced by a typical 1,000-megawatt commercial reactor
  • NASA Science, 'Moon Facts', 2026
    Some people call the far side – the hemisphere we never see from Earth – the "dark side", but that's misleading.

And the surface gets brutally cold, down to around minus two hundred eighty Fahrenheit.

  • NASA Science, 'Moon Facts', 2026
    Some people call the far side – the hemisphere we never see from Earth – the "dark side", but that's misleading.

On Earth, a few batteries get you through the night. On most of the Moon, running on solar alone means storing about two weeks of power. And those batteries have to be flown up from Earth. That isn't cheap. One NASA delivery contract came to roughly a million dollars for every kilogram of cargo it was hired to carry.

  • NASA press release (Feb. 4, 2021); CLPS Blue Ghost 1 press kit (2025), 2021
    approximately $93.3 million to deliver a suite of 10 science investigations ... collectively expected to total 207 pounds (94 kg)

In 2024, NASA ranked the technology it's missing. Number one was surviving the lunar night.

  • NASA, Directive on Fission Surface Power, 2025
    Minimum 100kWe power output / Assumed use of a heavy class lander (up to 15 metric tons mass available) / Readiness to launch by the first quarter of …

A reactor doesn't care where the Sun is. Once it's running, NASA says it can work for years without refueling.

  • NASA press release (Jan. 13, 2026; MOU added Feb. 2, 2026), 2026
    operate for years without the need to refuel

What's hiding in the dark?

In 2009, NASA crashed an empty rocket stage into a crater near the south pole. On purpose.

  • Colaprete vd., Science 330:463 (22.10.2010), 2010
    the concentration of water ice in the regolith at the LCROSS impact site is estimated to be 5.6 ± 2.9% by mass

A second spacecraft followed it down, watching the cloud it kicked up.

  • Colaprete vd., Science 330:463 (22.10.2010), 2010
    the concentration of water ice in the regolith at the LCROSS impact site is estimated to be 5.6 ± 2.9% by mass

There was water in it. The soil at that spot turned out to be an estimated five and a half percent ice, by weight.

  • Colaprete vd., Science 330:463 (22.10.2010), 2010
    the concentration of water ice in the regolith at the LCROSS impact site is estimated to be 5.6 ± 2.9% by mass

In 2018, scientists using a NASA instrument on an Indian spacecraft confirmed ice right at the surface, in scattered patches.

  • Li et al., PNAS 115(36):8907-8912 (online Aug. 20, 2018), 2018
    only ∼3.5% of cold traps exhibit ice exposures

It survives because the floors of those craters never see the Sun. Parts of them sit at around minus four hundred Fahrenheit.

  • NASA press release 09-215 (Sept. 17, 2009), 2009
    large areas in the permanently shadowed craters are about minus 400 degrees Fahrenheit (33 Kelvin)

So why does a little ice matter so much? Melt it, and you have water. Split it, and you have oxygen to breathe.

  • NASA Lunar Surface Technology page; FSP directive, 2026
    extracting water ice to convert to breathable oxygen

Every liter of water made on the Moon is one less liter to fly up from Earth. At that contract's rate, sending a single liter up there would cost about a million dollars.

  • NASA press release (Feb. 4, 2021); CLPS Blue Ghost 1 press kit (2025), 2021
    approximately $93.3 million to deliver a suite of 10 science investigations ... collectively expected to total 207 pounds (94 kg)

But here's the catch. The ice sits in permanent shadow, where solar panels are useless.

  • NASA press release 09-215 (Sept. 17, 2009), 2009
    large areas in the permanently shadowed craters are about minus 400 degrees Fahrenheit (33 Kelvin)

What happened in Canada in 1978?

Reactors in space aren't new, and the history is mixed. The Soviet Union flew more than thirty.

  • NASA TP-1999-208856 'Orbital Debris: A Chronology'; NASA ODPO (NTRS 20190033494), 1999
    Cosmos 198 is the first of 33 RORSATs launched up to March 1988.

The US flew one, in 1965.

  • DOE Office of Nuclear Energy; NASA SR-1 overview (March 2026), 1965
    the United States invested more than $20 billion across dozens of space nuclear programs and flew exactly one reactor — SNAP-10A, in 1965. It never left orbit.

In January 1978, one of the Soviet ones fell out of orbit.

  • Government of Canada, Statement of Claim against the USSR (JAXA space law archive copy), 1978-1981
    all but two of the fragments recovered were radioactive. Some fragments located proved to be of lethal radioactivity.

It came down over northern Canada, scattering pieces across the snow from the Northwest Territories into Alberta and Saskatchewan.

  • Government of Canada, Statement of Claim against the USSR (JAXA space law archive copy), 1978-1981
    all but two of the fragments recovered were radioactive. Some fragments located proved to be of lethal radioactivity.

Search teams went out onto the frozen ground with radiation detectors.

  • Government of Canada, Statement of Claim against the USSR (JAXA space law archive copy), 1978-1981
    all but two of the fragments recovered were radioactive. Some fragments located proved to be of lethal radioactivity.

Canada's claim against the Soviet Union put it plainly: nearly every piece they found was radioactive. Some were lethal.

  • Government of Canada, Statement of Claim against the USSR (JAXA space law archive copy), 1978-1981
    all but two of the fragments recovered were radioactive. Some fragments located proved to be of lethal radioactivity.

Canada billed the Soviet Union about six million Canadian dollars for the cleanup. It settled for three.

  • Canada–USSR Protocol (Moscow, April 2, 1981), UNOOSA bilateral agreements archive, 1981
    the sum of three million Canadian dollars (C$ 3,000,000.00) in full and final settlement of all matters connected with the disintegration of the Soviet satellite Cosmos 954 in …

That reactor had already been running in space before it fell.

  • Government of Canada, Statement of Claim against the USSR (JAXA space law archive copy), 1978-1981
    all but two of the fragments recovered were radioactive. Some fragments located proved to be of lethal radioactivity.

In 1992, the UN agreed on safety principles for reactors in space. One says a reactor shouldn't be switched on until it has reached its orbit, or is headed out into deep space.

  • BM Genel Kurulu (UNOOSA, ST/SPACE/51), 1992
    Nuclear reactors shall not be made critical before they have reached their operating orbit or interplanetary trajectory

NASA's Moon reactor follows the same idea. It launches switched off.

  • NASA (Helton, Mason, Forsbacka, NTRS 20230003898); SPARC Industry Day, 2023
    The reactor shall not be operated prior to space deployment, except for zero-power testing on the ground, for which negligible radioactivity is produced.

So if you're wondering what happens when a launch goes wrong: until it's switched on, its fuel is only mildly radioactive.

  • NASA (Helton, Mason, Forsbacka, NTRS 20230003898); SPARC Industry Day, 2023
    The reactor shall not be operated prior to space deployment, except for zero-power testing on the ground, for which negligible radioactivity is produced.

And the rules call for two separate ways to keep it from starting up by accident.

  • NASA (Helton, Mason, Forsbacka, NTRS 20230003898); SPARC Industry Day, 2023
    The reactor shall not be operated prior to space deployment, except for zero-power testing on the ground, for which negligible radioactivity is produced.

Nobody owns the Moon. So what's a keep-out zone?

Now back to that warning in NASA's order. What stops the first one there from fencing off the best ground?

  • NASA, Directive on Fission Surface Power, 2025
    The first country to do so could potentially declare a keep-out zone which would significantly inhibit the United States from establishing a planned Artemis presence if not there …

On paper, a treaty. In 1967, the space powers signed the Outer Space Treaty. The US, Russia, and China are all in it.

  • UN Office for Outer Space Affairs (UNOOSA), 1967
    Outer space, including the moon and other celestial bodies, is not subject to national appropriation by claim of sovereignty, by means of use or occupation, or by any …

No country can claim the Moon. Not by declaring it, not by using it, not by occupying it. And every country gets free access to every part of it.

  • UN Office for Outer Space Affairs (UNOOSA), 1967
    Outer space, including the moon and other celestial bodies, is not subject to national appropriation by claim of sovereignty, by means of use or occupation, or by any …
  • UNOOSA, 1967
    there shall be free access to all areas of celestial bodies

So how could anyone keep anyone out? In 2020, the US launched the Artemis Accords, a shared set of principles for exploring the Moon. Seventy-eight countries have signed.

  • NASA / U.S. Department of State (Artemis Accords text), 2020
    Safety zones will ultimately be temporary, ending when the relevant operation ceases
  • NASA Artemis Accords page and signatory list, 2026
    On October 28, 2026, Vietnam became the 78th nation to sign the accords.

China and Russia haven't. Their station has partners of its own.

  • Xinhua, gov.cn (23.4.2025; CNSA yetkilisi and Wu Weiren), 2025
    a basic model to be built by 2035 in the lunar south pole region, and an extended model to be built in the 2040s

One idea in them is a "safety zone": an area around your work where others should coordinate with you first. It's announced, and it ends when the work does.

  • NASA / U.S. Department of State (Artemis Accords text), 2020
    Safety zones will ultimately be temporary, ending when the relevant operation ceases

You can't own the lot. But you can mark off your yard and ask the neighbors to check with you first. And a reactor needs room: NASA's plan puts the base about a kilometer away from it.

Our calculations

  • "About a quarter of one percent": our calculation from NASA's LOLA average-illumination map (AVGVISIB_85S, 60 m per pixel, ground level). Of the 72,172 km² between 85°S and the pole, about 181 km² (0.25%) is sunlit at least half the time. The highest value, 88%, is on the ridge connecting Shackleton and de Gerlache craters. Ground level only: a few meters of height add sunlight (Mazarico et al. 2011).
  • "About the size of Washington, D.C.": D.C. covers about 177 km² including water (U.S. Census Bureau, 2020 Gazetteer: 158.3 km² land, 18.7 km² water).
  • "Roughly a million dollars for every kilogram of cargo": NASA's 2021 award to Firefly, about $93.3 million, for about 94 kg of planned science payload ≈ $0.99 million per kg. It is the whole-mission price of one contract (the contract later grew), not a freight rate.
  • "A single liter … about a million dollars": one liter of water weighs about one kilogram, priced at that one 2021 contract's rate. An illustration, not a quoted price for shipping water.
  • "An estimated five and a half percent ice": LCROSS measured 5.6 ± 2.9 percent water ice by mass in the impact plume (Colaprete et al. 2010).
  • "More than thirty" Soviet reactor satellites: 33 RORSAT launches through 1988 plus two TOPAZ test flights (NASA Orbital Debris Program Office).

Worth knowing

  • Power target: NASA's August 2025 directive asked for at least 100 kWe. The White House memo NSTM-3 (April 2026) set the mid-power reactors at at least 20 kWe, with at least one design able to scale to 100 kWe; NASA's LR-1 requirements say at least 20 kWe.
  • "Ready for launch by 2030" is NASA's stated goal for LR-1, not a landing date.
  • "Only opening this month": the final request for proposals for LR-1 was announced for on or about November 15, 2026 (SAM.gov, 80GRC026R0009). Checked again on the publication day.
  • Russia and China: the 2024 statement by Roscosmos's then-head Yuri Borisov, the May 2025 Roscosmos–CNSA memorandum and the December 2025 Roscosmos–Lavochkin contract (2025–2036) are reported by Interfax; the memorandum's text has not been published. A Rosatom figure of at least 5 kW appears in a single secondary source; we do not use it.
  • "Keep-out zone" is the wording of NASA's 2025 directive. We found no statement by China or Russia announcing such a zone. China's October 2026 position paper does not name the reactor or the Artemis Accords.
  • Chang'e-7's launch was postponed in August 2026; no new date or official landing coordinates have been published, so the maps show only the broad south-pole region of the China-led station (ILRS).
  • The Artemis Accords had 78 signatories as of October 28, 2026 (NASA's signatory list); China and Russia are not on it. Checked again on the publication day.
  • Price: NASA's budget does not list the lunar reactor as a separate line, and the Russian and Chinese sources we read give no project total. Hence "that we could find".
  • The 1992 UN Principles Relevant to the Use of Nuclear Power Sources in Outer Space (General Assembly resolution 47/68) are not a binding treaty.

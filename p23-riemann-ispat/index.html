So what does he claim to have proved? Count the prime numbers, and they thin out at an average rate we can predict. But the real count wobbles around it. In 1859, Bernhard Riemann guessed that the wobble stays about as small as it could be.

  • Clay Mathematics Institute, Riemann Hypothesis page, 2026
    The Riemann hypothesis tells us about the deviation from the average.

For a hundred and sixty-seven years, nobody could prove him right. Now Alpöge says he has, in a form only a machine can check in full.

The 27-line question

This has happened before. Eight days before the note, the mathematician Kevin Buzzard went through papers OpenAI had released with Lean code. In about thirty-eight, the code didn't even state the paper's main theorem. OpenAI never claimed it did.

  • Xena blogu, 12.11.2026, 2026
    the Lean code does not even state, let alone prove, the main theorem of the paper

But a computer can check every step perfectly, and still be checking the answer to a different question.

  • Xena Project blogu (Kevin Buzzard), 12.11.2026, 2026
    does the Lean statement match the informal statement?
  • arXiv 2610.08144, 2026
    the formalised Lean proof does not correspond to the NL proof of blow-up of solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations

So the community doesn't let the author write the question. They write it themselves, in twenty-seven lines.

  • Joint statement; Challenge.lean, 2026
    the theorem proved in Alpöge’s library is exactly Mathlib’s statement of the Riemann Hypothesis

Where the proof should go, it says "sorry." In Lean, that's the word for "proof missing."

Two separately written tools found an exact match.

But look at the first line of that file. "Import Mathlib." That's Lean's public math library. The twenty-seven lines don't define the Riemann hypothesis. They borrow the library's definition.

Eight thousand lines

Lean is built on an odd idea. Almost nothing in it is trusted. Not the person who wrote the proof. Not the AI.

  • Leonardo de Moura (Lean FRO), postmortem 1.8.2026, 2026
    The elaborator is untrusted by design.

Everything they produce is broken into tiny steps and passed to one program, the kernel.

  • Leonardo de Moura (Lean FRO), postmortem 1.8.2026, 2026
    The elaborator is untrusted by design.

The kernel is about eight thousand lines long.

  • Our own count, github.com/leanprover/lean4 @v4.33.1, 2026 partly confirmed

It can't find a proof. It only checks that each step follows the rules. So you don't have to trust seventeen million lines. You have to trust eight thousand.

  • Our own count, github.com/leanprover/lean4 @v4.33.1, 2026 partly confirmed
  • leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis, NOTES.md 'Size', 2026

The day the checker was fooled

They do have bugs. In July, an AI-assisted proof claimed to disprove another famous conjecture. It had no "sorry." And the kernel accepted it.

  • Leonardo de Moura, postmortem 1.8.2026, 2026
    It is not a valid proof because it exploits a bug in the kernel's handling of nested inductive types.

The proof was false. It had found a bug in the kernel, and walked through it.

  • Leonardo de Moura, postmortem 1.8.2026, 2026
    It is not a valid proof because it exploits a bug in the kernel's handling of nested inductive types.

An old version of a second, independent checker let it through too, by a different bug.

  • Leonardo de Moura, postmortem 1.8.2026, 2026
    It is not a valid proof because it exploits a bug in the kernel's handling of nested inductive types.

And the kernel isn't the bottom. It runs on a chip.

  • Thomas R. Nicely, pentbug.html (Internet Archive copy), 1994
    isolated to the twin-prime pair (824633702441, 824633702443)

In 1994, a mathematician named Thomas Nicely was running a long calculation on prime numbers when his results stopped agreeing. His Pentium chip was dividing wrong.

  • Thomas R. Nicely, pentbug.html (Internet Archive copy), 1994
    isolated to the twin-prime pair (824633702441, 824633702443)

That cost Intel four hundred and seventy-five million dollars.

  • Intel 10-K, fiscal 1994 (SEC, Mar 28, 1995), 1994
    a $475 million charge, primarily to cost of sales

So the real list is longer than eight thousand lines. The compiler that builds the kernel. The chip underneath. The tool that exports the proof. The library's definitions. Any of them can be wrong.

  • Lean Language Reference, 'Validating a Lean Proof', 2026
    Implementation bugs present in some (but not simultaneously in all) of the used checkers.
  • de Moura, 15.10.2026, 2026
    It is very unlikely that the same bug also exists in the Rust compiler or runtime

Eight hours later, an update. Readers had found problems in it, with the help of AI models.

  • Ravi Vakil, math216.wordpress.com, 20.11.2026, 2026

Where the proof stands

On November twenty-first, the Lean and Mathlib communities wrote that they had "full confidence" the proof is correct.

  • Lean community blog, 'Lean verification of the Riemann Hypothesis', 2026
    We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct

The million dollars is further off. The prize rules ask for a published solution, then at least two years, then general acceptance by mathematicians. That clock hasn't started.

  • Clay Mathematics Institute, Millennium Prize rules (26.9.2018), 2018
    at least two (2) years have elapsed since publication of the Proposed Solution in a Qualifying Outlet

So how do you check a proof nobody can read? You write the question yourself. You hand the answer to one small program. Then you refuse to trust only one. If Lean, and the tools under it, are sound. If the library's definition is what Riemann meant. If no single flaw runs through every checker. Then it's proved, and nobody had to understand why.

  • Lean Language Reference, 'Validating a Lean Proof', 2026
    Implementation bugs present in some (but not simultaneously in all) of the used checkers.
  • Mathlib, Mathlib/NumberTheory/LSeries/RiemannZeta.lean, 2024
    constructing a term of this type is worth a million dollars

80 feet under Manhattan

Stand on Liberty Street in lower Manhattan and look down. Eighty feet below you, under the New York Fed's building, on the island's bedrock, is a vault.

  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York
    80 feet below street level and 50 feet below sea level

It calls itself the world's largest known store of monetary gold.

  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York, 2024
    the world's largest known depository of monetary gold

None of that gold belongs to the Fed.

  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York
    None of the gold stored in the vault belongs to the New York Fed

It belongs to governments, central banks and a few international organizations. Germany's share is 1,236 metric tons.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (key figures), 2025

That's about a fifth of the vault.

It's also more than a third of all the gold Germany owns.

    1236 / 3350

And Germany pays no rent for the space.

  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York

How did Germany end up with 3,350 tons?

Start with a surprise. In the five years after the war, Germany had no gold reserves at all.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (development of gold reserves page), 1945-1952
    During the post-war years from 1945 to 1950, Germany did not hold any gold reserves.

Now imagine you work the docks in Hamburg in the late 1950s. Crates of machines and cars are going out. West Germany is selling more than it buys, year after year, and payment piles up.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (development of gold reserves page), 1951-1968
    Thanks to thriving exports, Germany recorded an increasing current account surplus" · "tying the dollar to gold ($35 per troy ounce)
  • Deutsche Bundesbank (development of gold reserves page), 1945-1952
    During the post-war years from 1945 to 1950, Germany did not hold any gold reserves.

Back then, money had an anchor. Under a system called Bretton Woods, the dollar was tied to gold, at 35 dollars an ounce. A central bank could hand its dollars to the United States and get gold back.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (development of gold reserves page), 1951-1968
    Thanks to thriving exports, Germany recorded an increasing current account surplus" · "tying the dollar to gold ($35 per troy ounce)

So West Germany's central bank turned part of that surplus into gold.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (development of gold reserves page), 1951-1968
    Thanks to thriving exports, Germany recorded an increasing current account surplus" · "tying the dollar to gold ($35 per troy ounce)
  • Deutsche Bundesbank (speech, 21 Sep 2022) + development of gold reserves page, 1950
    das Gold wurde direkt in die ausländischen Goldlagerstellen, etwa in London oder Paris, verbracht

But the gold didn't come home. It was handed over in vaults abroad, at the New York Fed, the Bank of England and elsewhere.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (speech, 21 Sep 2022) + development of gold reserves page, 1950
    das Gold wurde direkt in die ausländischen Goldlagerstellen, etwa in London oder Paris, verbracht

By 1968, the country that had started with nothing held about 4,000 tons.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (speech, 2022), 1968
    Höchststand von 4.033 Tonnen

Some was sold to the U.S. Federal Reserve in 1969.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (development of gold reserves page), 1969
    In 1969, the Bundesbank sold gold reserves to the Fed, reducing its holdings by 14 million ounces.

Some later went to the European Central Bank, and some was minted into coins.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (development of gold reserves page) + Bundestag WD 4-3000-022/19, 1999
    consisted of 15% physical gold (7.46 million ounces) to the ECB" · "rund 232 Tonnen an die Europäische Zentralbank (EZB)
  • Deutscher Bundestag, Wissenschaftliche Dienste WD 4-022-19, 2001-2018
    Für die Prägung von Sammlermünzen (seit 2001) (ca. 99 Tonnen).

Why not bring it home?

New York was already where much of the world kept its gold safe. During and after the Second World War, country after country sent its reserves there to keep them safe.

  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York, 1939-1973
    as many countries wanted to store their gold reserves in a safe location

If you ran West Germany's central bank in the 1960s, the reason to leave your gold there was on the map. Soviet armies stood on the other side of the Iron Curtain. Gold in New York was out of their reach.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (speech, 2022); AFP 2018
    Während der Zeit des Kalten Krieges war es durchaus sinnvoll, dass die Bundesbank die ihr zufließenden Goldbestände weit westlich bei ihren Verbündeten beließ

The Bundesbank gives a second reason, one it still uses today: speed. In a crisis, a country may need dollars fast. Gold already sitting in New York can be swapped for them without crossing an ocean first.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (statement, 22 Oct 2012)
    exchanged, if necessary, into a commonly used reserve currency without any logistical constraints" · "the book inventory, in use for years, and the accompanying confirmations by the depositories

1967: a letter to Washington

If you held dollars back then, you had a choice: keep them, or ask for gold. France asked. Under Charles de Gaulle, France's central bank turned more and more of its dollars into gold. In 1965, de Gaulle publicly called for a return to the gold standard.

  • NBER w24016; Avaro (Penn), 1965 partly confirmed

Germany chose the opposite. In 1967, Bundesbank president Karl Blessing wrote to the head of the Federal Reserve. In effect, he promised that Germany would not turn its dollars into gold on a large scale.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (archive), 1967
    Karl Blessing promises, in effect, to refrain from a large-scale dollar conversion into gold.

Why? Blessing later said one reason was fear that American troops might leave Germany.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (archive), 1967
    the withdrawal of American troops from Germany, had been one of the factors that led him to give in to US demands

Historians still disagree on how much the letter gave away.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (archive), 1967
    Karl Blessing promises, in effect, to refrain from a large-scale dollar conversion into gold.

Blessing died in April 1971. In an interview published days later, he said he should have been more rigorous with the United States.

  • Deutsche Bundesbank (archive, quoting Der Spiegel), 1971
    I should have been more rigorous with regard to the US.
  • Deutsche Biographie + Bundesbank archive (footnote 8), 1971
    Gestorben am 25. April 1971 in Rasteau" · "published 3 May 1971

A few months later, the United States stopped swapping dollars for gold altogether. Foreign governments could no longer turn their dollars in.

  • Federal Reserve History, 1971
    Foreign governments could no longer exchange their dollars for gold.

A storage unit with no rent

So what does Germany actually get in New York? Picture a storage unit resting on the bedrock, deep enough that its floor sits fifty feet below sea level.

  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York
    80 feet below street level and 50 feet below sea level

There is one way in: through a 90-ton steel cylinder.

  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York
    a 90-ton steel cylinder protecting the only entry into the vault

Once it closes, time locks keep it shut until the next business day.

  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York
    a 90-ton steel cylinder protecting the only entry into the vault

Each owner's gold sits in its own compartment, behind a padlock, two combination locks and an auditor's seal.

  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York
  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York
    a padlock, two combination locks and an auditor's seal

Each bar weighs about 27 pounds.

  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York
    Each gold bar weighs approximately 27 pounds

Whenever a compartment is opened, three people have to be there. Two vault staff and one of the Fed's own auditors.

  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York
    whenever gold is moved or a compartment is opened in the vault—even to change a light bulb

Even to change a light bulb.

  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York
    whenever gold is moved or a compartment is opened in the vault—even to change a light bulb

The Fed doesn't charge for storage. It charges a handling fee when gold comes in, goes out, or changes owner.

  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York
    does not charge fees for gold storage

Think of a storage unit with no rent, where you only pay when you move a box. But there's a catch. The building belongs to someone else, more than 6,000 kilometers from Frankfurt.

2012: has anyone counted it?

If it were your savings, you'd want to count them. In 2012, Germany's Federal Court of Auditors asked exactly that. The bars abroad, it said, had never been physically checked, not by the Bundesbank itself, and not by independent auditors.

  • Bundesrechnungshof (AP metni, 22 Oct 2012), 2012
    have never been physically checked by the Bundesbank itself or other independent auditors

The Bundesbank pushed back. Audits like that, it said, are not common practice among central banks.

  • Bundesrechnungshof (AP metni, 22 Oct 2012), 2012
    have never been physically checked by the Bundesbank itself or other independent auditors
  • Deutsche Bundesbank (statement, 22 Oct 2012)
    exchanged, if necessary, into a commonly used reserve currency without any logistical constraints" · "the book inventory, in use for years, and the accompanying confirmations by the depositories

One of its board members later said he had gone down into the vault in 2012 and watched bars, picked by the bank, being pulled out and reweighed.

  • Handelsblatt interview with C.-L. Thiele (Bundesbank translation, via mining.com, Feb 2014), 2012
    a number of bars selected by us were removed, inspected and reweighed even while I was there" · "I was in New York myself in June 2012

And over the years, the bank has sample-tested 13 percent of the New York stock.

  • Reuters (Kitco, 30 May 2025), 2025
    had inspected 13% of stock in New York over the years

So German staff have checked some of the bars themselves. For the rest, the bank relies on the Fed's records and written confirmations.

France sold its New York gold

If you lived in Germany in 2025, you would have heard the old question again. As relations with Washington grew tense, some German politicians and economists called for the gold to come home.

  • Kitco; Reuters, 2025-2026 partly confirmed

Moving it, Reuters noted, could be read as a lack of confidence in the Fed.

  • Reuters (Kitco, 30 May 2025), 2025
    could be interpreted as lack of confidence in the Federal Reserve

France had a different starting point. It already kept almost all of its gold at home. About five percent was still in New York.

  • Banque de France (press release) + Börsen-Zeitung (28 Apr 2026) + IBTimes, 2025
    a residual portion (5%)

Its central bank sold those last 129 tons there. It took about six months, and it bought new bars in Europe.

  • Banque de France (BDOR / IBTimes via), 2025-2026 partly confirmed

The bank called the move technical: the old bars didn't meet today's standards.

  • Banque de France (press release) + Kitco (Villeroy), 2026
    align a residual portion (5%) with technical guidelines" · Villeroy: "not politically motivated" (Kitco)

Because the old bars were sold at today's prices, the bank also booked a gain of 11 billion euros in 2025.

  • Banque de France (25 Mar 2026 press releasei), 2025-2026
    This exceptional foreign exchange income totalled EUR 11 billion for 2025." · "while the volume of gold reserves remained unchanged

Its total gold stayed the same.

  • Banque de France (25 Mar 2026 press releasei), 2025-2026
    This exceptional foreign exchange income totalled EUR 11 billion for 2025." · "while the volume of gold reserves remained unchanged

All of France's gold now sits in Paris.

  • press reports, 2026 partly confirmed

The Dutch central bank went partway. In 2026, it cut its New York share to under a fifth, and moved the difference to London.

  • De Nederlandsche Bank, 2026
    In view of increasing geopolitical unrest, DNB is strengthening its crisis preparedness.

Germany's answer was to stay. In January 2026, Bundesbank president Joachim Nagel said he had no doubt the gold was safe in New York.

  • dpa (cash-online, 28 Jan 2026), 2025-2026

The bank calls the New York Fed a trustworthy, reliable partner.

  • Reuters (Kitco, 30 May 2025), 2025

So why does Germany keep its gold under Manhattan? Because much of it was handed over there. Because in the Cold War, it was safer there. And because, the Bundesbank says, it's useful there in a crisis. What does it cost? Nothing in rent. The price is the door. Our calculations

  • Share of Germany's gold in New York: 1,236 ÷ 3,350 = 36.9% (Bundesbank, end of 2025).
  • Germany's share of the New York Fed vault: 1,236 ÷ 6,331 = 19.5%, "about a fifth" (Bundesbank and New York Fed figures; the vault total is for 2024, the German total for end of 2025 and end of 2024 alike).
  • 1968 peak: 129.69 million troy ounces ≈ 4,034 metric tons; the Bundesbank's own speech gives 4,033 tonnes. 1969 sale to the Fed: 14 million ounces ≈ 435 metric tons (31.1035 g per troy ounce).
  • Frankfurt to New York: about 6,200 km great-circle distance (our calculation). 80 feet ≈ 24 m; 50 feet ≈ 15 m; 27 pounds ≈ 12 kg.

Worth knowing

  • "No rent" means the New York Fed charges no storage fee; it does charge a handling fee when gold moves in, out or between owners.
  • The 1967 Blessing letter concerned converting dollars into gold, not where Germany's gold is stored. Historians disagree on how much it gave away.
  • France's gain of €11 billion in 2025 is the Banque de France's realised currency gain on selling older bars; its total gold holdings stayed the same.
  • We do not suggest the gold is missing. The Federal Court of Auditors' 2012 criticism and the Bundesbank's reply and sample checks are both reported.

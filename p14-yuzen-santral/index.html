Why Russia Parked a Floating Nuclear Plant in the Arctic

Geonomics. Every narrated claim with its source and a short quote in the original language. Errors? Say so in the video's comments; corrections are pinned.

Why park a nuclear plant next to 4,000 people?

This is Pevek, Russia's northernmost town, in the polar night.

  • Russian Ministry of Transport (Mintrans), news release, Sept. 27, 2023, 2023
    Певек – это райцентр Чаунского района Чукотского автономного округа (ЧАО) и самый северный город России.

About four thousand people live here.

  • Rosstat, 2020 All-Russian Population Census (Oct. 1, 2021), vol. 1, table 5, 2021
    Городское население - г. Певек 4015

And moored at its edge is a nuclear power plant that floats.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "Get the facts" (archived), 2019-2022
    without any in-built motion capabilities (no motor or propeller functions)
  • Rosatom, fnpp.info news, Aug. 23, 2019, and summary, Sept. 13, 2024, 2019
    Upon completing the 4,700 km journey to Pevek

So why did Russia park a floating nuclear plant in the Arctic, beside a town this small? It has no engine. Tugboats had to drag it here, across the top of Russia.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "Get the facts" (archived), 2019-2022
    without any in-built motion capabilities (no motor or propeller functions)
  • Bloomberg (via Insurance Journal), O. Tanas, Aug. 23, 2019, 2016
    was estimated at almost 30 billion rubles ($458 million)
  • Rosatom, fnpp.info news, Aug. 23, 2019, and summary, Sept. 13, 2024, 2019
    Upon completing the 4,700 km journey to Pevek

And its owner, the state nuclear company Rosatom, says it could power a city of up to a hundred thousand.

  • Rosatom media center, press release, Dec. 19, 2024, 2024
    в 2024 – уже 250 млн кВтч

Pevek isn't close to that size. So the town can't be the whole answer. And it isn't. This plant was also built for customers you can't see from the dock: mines, far inland. The biggest one hasn't even opened yet.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info news, Jan. 16, 2025, 2025
    One of its main tasks in the region is supplying energy for the large-scale mining operations at the main deposits in the Baimskaya ore area.
  • Kommersant, July 9, 2026, 2026
    KAZ Minerals вышла из проекта в 2023 году, передав актив компании Trianon Limited

One more thing. You may have heard it called the world's first floating nuclear plant. It isn't. As far as we can find, it's the only one running today.

  • World Nuclear News, "An unsung hero makes its last voyage" (2015), 2015
    the first floating nuclear plant to supply an onshore grid
  • Bellona; oenergetice.cz; IAEA PRIS search (Geonomics, Nov. 2026), 2026 partly confirmed

How cut off is Pevek?

Start with the map. Pevek sits on the Arctic coast of Chukotka, in the far northeastern corner of Russia.

  • Russian Ministry of Transport (Mintrans), news release, Sept. 27, 2023, 2023
    Певек – это райцентр Чаунского района Чукотского автономного округа (ЧАО) и самый северный город России.

Its power grid isn't connected to the rest of the country.

  • Rosatom media center, press release, Aug. 19, 2024 (quoting the System Operator, Far East), 2024
    линия электропередачи напряжением 110 киловольт протяженностью 490 километров, которая связала города Певек и Билибино

Think of it as an island. Every bit of electricity used on it has to be made on it. And getting anything in is hard. The sea in front of Pevek is normally open to ships for about four months a year, from July to October.

  • Mintrans order No. 298 (Oct. 13, 2015), mandatory rules for the port of Pevek, 2015
    Навигация в морском порту осуществляется в период с июля по октябрь.

Even the plant's new nuclear fuel comes in by ship, in the summer.

  • Rosenergoatom, IAEA conference paper, 2024
    The preferred delivery method in 2023 was chosen to be combined delivery by sea transport
  • TVEL (Rosatom fuel company), press release via atommedia, Aug. 17, 2026, 2026
    The next scheduled refueling for the second FNPP reactor is slated for 2027.

Before the floating plant, two old machines kept the lights on. A coal plant in Pevek, more than seventy years old.

  • Rosatom media center, press release, Aug. 19, 2024 (quoting the System Operator, Far East), 2024
    линия электропередачи напряжением 110 киловольт протяженностью 490 километров, которая связала города Певек и Билибино
  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "About" and "Get the facts", 2019-2022
    will replace Pevek’s aging Bilibino nuclear power plant and Chaunsk coal-fired power plant

And a small nuclear station inland, at Bilibino, running since 1974.

  • IAEA PRIS (via WNA), Bilibino-1, 2026
    Permanent Shutdown 14 January 2019

Its last reactor shut down for good at the end of 2025, after fifty-one years of operation.

  • PRIME news agency, Dec. 30, 2025 (citing Rosenergoatom), 2025
    Билибинская АЭС полностью остановлена после 51 года эксплуатации

So Chukotka needed a new power plant, in a place ships can normally reach only about four months a year.

  • Mintrans order No. 298 (Oct. 13, 2015), mandatory rules for the port of Pevek, 2015
    Навигация в морском порту осуществляется в период с июля по октябрь.
  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "About" and "Get the facts", 2019-2022
    will replace Pevek’s aging Bilibino nuclear power plant and Chaunsk coal-fired power plant

Why make it float?

That's the idea. Build the power plant in a shipyard, where the cranes and the workers already are. Then tow it to wherever it's needed.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "Get the facts"; OKBM Afrikantov presentation, 2024, 2019-2022
    the floating design of the power plant lowers capital cost construction in the area where the nuclear power plant (NPP) is located

On site, you mostly need a pier, a breakwater, and connections for power and heat.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "Get the facts"; OKBM Afrikantov presentation, 2024, 2019-2022
    the floating design of the power plant lowers capital cost construction in the area where the nuclear power plant (NPP) is located

What they built is called the Akademik Lomonosov. It's a barge a hundred and forty-four meters long, longer than a football field.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "About" (archived), 2019-2022
    the vessel is 144 metres long and 30 metres wide, and has a displacement of 21,000 tonnes

It weighs about twenty-one thousand tons.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "About" (archived), 2019-2022
    the vessel is 144 metres long and 30 metres wide, and has a displacement of 21,000 tonnes

Inside are two reactors, the kind that push Russia's nuclear icebreakers.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "About" (archived), 2019-2022
    the vessel is 144 metres long and 30 metres wide, and has a displacement of 21,000 tonnes
  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "Get the facts"; OKBM Afrikantov presentation, 2024, 2019-2022
    the floating design of the power plant lowers capital cost construction in the area where the nuclear power plant (NPP) is located

Their power goes ashore as electricity, and as heat for the town's radiators.

  • Rosenergoatom (Beltyukov, Markov, Toropov), "Experience of FNPP operation", IAEA conference paper, 2024
    The FNPP is capable of delivering more than 60 MWe to the grid with its auxiliaries being 7.1 MW.
  • Rosatom media center (atommedia), press releases, Jan. 31 and Dec. 19, 2024, 2024
    около 70 МВт, а в режиме выдачи максимальной тепловой мощности – порядка 44 МВт

And remember, no engine. Once it's moored, it stays. It's less a ship than a building that happens to float, designed to work for forty years.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "Get the facts" (archived), 2019-2022
    without any in-built motion capabilities (no motor or propeller functions)
  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "About" (archived), 2019-2022
    the vessel is 144 metres long and 30 metres wide, and has a displacement of 21,000 tonnes

Where does the used fuel go?

A reactor like this doesn't get topped up. Every three years or so, the whole core is swapped at once, right where the plant is moored.

  • Rosenergoatom, IAEA conference paper; Rosatom, fnpp.info, 2024
    The refueling interval according to the reactor operation model is from 2.5 to 3 years.
  • TVEL (Rosatom fuel company), press release via atommedia, Aug. 17, 2026, 2026
    The next scheduled refueling for the second FNPP reactor is slated for 2027.

Both reactors have already had a new core.

  • TVEL (Rosatom fuel company), press release via atommedia, Aug. 17, 2026, 2026
    The next scheduled refueling for the second FNPP reactor is slated for 2027.

And the used fuel? You might expect it to go ashore. It doesn't. It stays on board.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "Get the facts", 2019-2022
    The unloaded fuel will remain on board the FNPP. ... at no point will it be taken ashore.

The original plan had an answer for that. Every ten to twelve years, the plant would be towed away for a major overhaul, and unloaded there.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "Get the facts", 2019-2022
    The unloaded fuel will remain on board the FNPP. ... at no point will it be taken ashore.
  • Rosenergoatom, IAEA conference paper, 2024
    Due to the absence of replacement capacities for the FNPP during the FPU intermediate-level maintenance, there are plans to conduct this maintenance at its permanent base in the …

But there's a catch. If the plant leaves, nothing can replace it.

  • Rosenergoatom, IAEA conference paper, 2024
    Due to the absence of replacement capacities for the FNPP during the FPU intermediate-level maintenance, there are plans to conduct this maintenance at its permanent base in the …

So the plan changed. The overhaul is now supposed to happen in Pevek, while the plant keeps running.

  • Rosenergoatom, IAEA conference paper, 2024
    Due to the absence of replacement capacities for the FNPP during the FPU intermediate-level maintenance, there are plans to conduct this maintenance at its permanent base in the …

And the plan includes a special service ship, to help take away used fuel and waste.

  • Rosenergoatom, IAEA conference paper, 2024
    Due to the absence of replacement capacities for the FNPP during the FPU intermediate-level maintenance, there are plans to conduct this maintenance at its permanent base in the …

Why did it leave without fuel?

Rewind to St. Petersburg, where the plant was built. The original plan was to load its nuclear fuel right there, in the city.

  • Bellona (Norwegian environmental group), April 30, 2018, 2018
    Public opposition and intervention from Norway's foreign ministry
  • Greenpeace International, Jan Haverkamp, "32 years on: Chornobyl on ice?" (2018), 2018
    the next nuclear catastrophe could well be a Chornobyl on ice or a Chornobyl on-the-rocks

According to Greenpeace, twelve thousand people in the city signed a petition against it.

  • Greenpeace International, Jan Haverkamp, "32 years on: Chornobyl on ice?" (2018), 2018
    the next nuclear catastrophe could well be a Chornobyl on ice or a Chornobyl on-the-rocks

And Greenpeace warned that if this development wasn't stopped, the next nuclear catastrophe could well be "a Chornobyl on ice."

  • Greenpeace International, Jan Haverkamp, "32 years on: Chornobyl on ice?" (2018), 2018
    the next nuclear catastrophe could well be a Chornobyl on ice or a Chornobyl on-the-rocks

In the end, the fueling was moved. In April 2018, the plant left St. Petersburg empty. No fuel on board.

  • Bellona (Norwegian environmental group), April 30, 2018, 2018
    Public opposition and intervention from Norway's foreign ministry

It was towed around Scandinavia to Murmansk, and fueled there instead.

  • PRIME news agency, May 19, 2018 (citing a Rosatom official), 2018
    прибыл с Балтийского завода и встал у причальной стенки в Мурманске

That's where its reactors were started for the first time, late in 2018.

  • IAEA PRIS (via the World Nuclear Association reactor database), 2026
    Construction Start 15 April 2007 ... First Grid Connection 19 December 2019

So you might ask: is it safe? Rosatom says the hull has ten watertight compartments, and stays afloat even if two of them flood. It says a breakwater shields the plant from tsunamis and ice.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "Get the facts", 2019-2022
    The FNPP is divided into 10 watertight compartments and will remain afloat even if two of them are flooded

Those are the company's own claims.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "Get the facts", 2019-2022
    The FNPP is divided into 10 watertight compartments and will remain afloat even if two of them are flooded

Here's what is on the record. In its first years, the plant's steam generators leaked more often than the design expected. Parts were replaced in 2023.

  • Rosenergoatom, IAEA conference paper, 2024
    a beyond-design number of leaks occured in the tube systems of the internal devices of the steam generators

No radiation release was reported.

  • Rosenergoatom, IAEA conference paper, 2024
    a beyond-design number of leaks occured in the tube systems of the internal devices of the steam generators

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info news, Aug. 23, 2019, and summary, Sept. 13, 2024, 2019

Who was it really built for?

Go back to that claim: enough for a city of a hundred thousand.

  • Rosatom media center, press release, Dec. 19, 2024, 2024
    в 2024 – уже 250 млн кВтч

In practice, it hasn't been working anywhere near that hard. Over its first six years, by our math, it averaged roughly a third of its full electrical output.

  • IAEA PRIS (via WNA), Akademik Lomonosov-1 and -2, 2020-2025
    Electricity Supplied (GWh) … 2024 144.91
  • Geonomics calculation (see Calculations below), 2026

The operator's own explanation is on the map. The power lines and the industrial customers it was designed for weren't there yet.

  • Rosenergoatom, IAEA conference paper, 2024
    Operational performance indicators of the FNPP from 2020 to 2024 were below the designed operation model due to the need to introduce new transit lines and the load …

A new long line, nearly five hundred kilometers to Bilibino, only opened in 2023.

  • Rosatom media center, press release, Aug. 19, 2024 (quoting the System Operator, Far East), 2024
    линия электропередачи напряжением 110 киловольт протяженностью 490 километров, которая связала города Певек и Билибино

And the customers? Read the plant's own job description. One of its main tasks is supplying energy for large-scale mining.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info news, Jan. 16, 2025, 2025
    One of its main tasks in the region is supplying energy for the large-scale mining operations at the main deposits in the Baimskaya ore area.

Above all, that means one giant copper and gold project called Baimskaya. It's an eight-and-a-half-billion-dollar plan, now due to open in 2029.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info news, Jan. 16, 2025, 2025
    One of its main tasks in the region is supplying energy for the large-scale mining operations at the main deposits in the Baimskaya ore area.
  • Kommersant, July 9, 2026, 2026
    KAZ Minerals вышла из проекта в 2023 году, передав актив компании Trianon Limited

That mine needs far more power than this one plant can give. So Rosatom is building four more floating units for it. The first is now expected around 2028.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info news, July 23, 2021, 2021
    the first two units are expected to be delivered to their working location in the water area of Cape Nagleynyn … at the end of 2026
  • IAEA PRIS (via WNA), Cape Nagloynyn-1; World Nuclear News, Aug. 31, 2022, 2022
    Construction Start 31 August 2022
  • Nuclear Engineering International, Sept. 25, 2026; 3dnews, May 27, 2026 (citing Rosatom), 2026 partly confirmed
    scheduled for commissioning in 2028

So here's the answer. Russia didn't park a nuclear plant here just for four thousand people. It parked it here to replace two aging power plants, and to power a grid that's meant to reach the mines.

  • Rosatom media center, press release, Aug. 19, 2024 (quoting the System Operator, Far East), 2024
    линия электропередачи напряжением 110 киловольт протяженностью 490 километров, которая связала города Певек и Билибино
  • Rosatom, fnpp.info news, Jan. 16, 2025, 2025
    One of its main tasks in the region is supplying energy for the large-scale mining operations at the main deposits in the Baimskaya ore area.

What did it cost, and who pays?

Now the money. Start with the price of the plant itself. In 2006, the estimate was about nine billion rubles. By 2015, a reported estimate was roughly four times that, before adjusting for inflation.

  • Rossiyskaya Gazeta, May 15, 2015 (citing a draft government decree), 2006
    ее общая стоимость с учетом этих ассигнований составит 37,3 миллиарда рублей

And the final bill? Rosatom hasn't published it.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "Get the facts", 2019-2022
    Overall costs of the “Akademik Lomonosov” FNPP have not been disclosed

Outside estimates in 2018 and 2019 put it near half a billion dollars.

  • Bloomberg (via Insurance Journal), O. Tanas, Aug. 23, 2019, 2016
    was estimated at almost 30 billion rubles ($458 million)
  • Bellona (Norwegian environmental group), April 30, 2018, 2018
    Public opposition and intervention from Norway's foreign ministry

The plan was for Rosatom's power company to fund it from its own reserves, with federal money for the shore works.

  • Rossiyskaya Gazeta (Russian government newspaper), May 15, 2015, 2015
    концерн «Росэнергоатом» как заказчик и основной инвестор вложит в проект собственные средства из резерва развития
  • Rossiyskaya Gazeta, May 15, 2015 (citing a draft government decree), 2006
    ее общая стоимость с учетом этих ассигнований составит 37,3 миллиарда рублей

The more interesting question is who pays for the electricity. Power is expensive out here. In 2025, Chukotka's official full-cost rate for businesses was more than forty rubles a kilowatt-hour. That's for the whole regional grid, not this plant alone.

  • Chukotka Committee for Price and Tariff Regulation, protocol No. 23 (Dec. 10, 2024), tariff annex, 2025
    Экономически обоснованные тарифы … 41,63

Businesses that qualify for a discount paid less than a quarter of that.

  • Chukotka Committee for Price and Tariff Regulation, protocol No. 23 (Dec. 10, 2024), tariff annex, 2025
    Экономически обоснованные тарифы … 41,63

So who covers the gap? A surcharge, added to power prices across most of the rest of Russia: the European part, the Urals, Siberia. That money is sent east.

  • Government of Russia, decree No. 895 (July 28, 2017), 2017
    надбавки к цене на мощность, поставляемую в ценовых зонах оптового рынка

To be clear, this isn't a special deal for Pevek. The same rule covers Russia's whole Far East.

  • Government of Russia, decree No. 895 (July 28, 2017), 2017
    надбавки к цене на мощность, поставляемую в ценовых зонах оптового рынка

It's a discount for power users, not a payment for building the plant. We couldn't confirm how its construction cost is recovered.

  • Government of Russia, decree No. 895 (July 28, 2017), 2017
    надбавки к цене на мощность, поставляемую в ценовых зонах оптового рынка

In short, power users across most of Russia help pay for cheaper electricity in Chukotka.

  • Chukotka Committee for Price and Tariff Regulation, protocol No. 23 (Dec. 10, 2024), tariff annex, 2025

  • Government of Russia, decree No. 895 (July 28, 2017), 2017

What happened to the first one?

For that, there's one finished example, and it isn't Russian. You've probably never heard of it.

  • U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) Baltimore District, STURGIS fact sheet (Feb. 1, 2015), 2015
    The vessel, which essentially became a barge, was renamed STURGIS.
  • USACE fact sheet (2015) and project page, 2015
    the STURGIS was towed 1,750 miles from Virginia to Galveston, Texas in April 2015

In the 1960s, the U.S. Army took a World War II cargo ship, pulled out its engine, and built a nuclear reactor into its middle.

  • U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) Baltimore District, STURGIS fact sheet (Feb. 1, 2015), 2015
    The vessel, which essentially became a barge, was renamed STURGIS.

They renamed it the Sturgis. In 1968, it was moored in the Panama Canal Zone.

  • U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) Baltimore District, STURGIS fact sheet (Feb. 1, 2015), 2015
    The vessel, which essentially became a barge, was renamed STURGIS.
  • USACE Baltimore District, STURGIS project page (archived), 2026
    The MH-1A was used to generate electricity in the Panama Canal Zone from 1968 to 1976.

The canal was short of water that year. And the same lake that fills the locks also ran a hydroelectric dam.

  • University of Florida, Panama Canal Museum Collection (USACE Office of History photos and oral history), 2023 partly confirmed
    helped reserve over one trillion gallons of water for use in Canal operations

So the Sturgis made the electricity instead, and the lake's water could go to the ships.

  • University of Florida, Panama Canal Museum Collection (USACE Office of History photos and oral history), 2023 partly confirmed
    helped reserve over one trillion gallons of water for use in Canal operations

That October, it became the first floating nuclear plant to power a grid on shore. It made about a seventh of the Russian plant's power.

  • Rosatom, fnpp.info, "About" (archived), 2019-2022
    the vessel is 144 metres long and 30 metres wide, and has a displacement of 21,000 tonnes
  • World Nuclear News, "An unsung hero makes its last voyage" (2015), 2015
    the first floating nuclear plant to supply an onshore grid

It worked for eight years. Then the canal's power system got new turbines, and the Sturgis wasn't needed.

  • USACE Baltimore District, STURGIS project page (archived), 2026
    The MH-1A was used to generate electricity in the Panama Canal Zone from 1968 to 1976.
  • University of Florida, Panama Canal Museum Collection (USACE Office of History photos and oral history), 2023 partly confirmed
    helped reserve over one trillion gallons of water for use in Canal operations

Its fuel was removed. The ship was sealed and parked in a reserve fleet in Virginia. It sat there for thirty-seven years.

  • USACE Baltimore District, STURGIS project page (archived), 2026
    The MH-1A was used to generate electricity in the Panama Canal Zone from 1968 to 1976.
  • USACE fact sheet (2015) and project page, 2015
    the STURGIS was towed 1,750 miles from Virginia to Galveston, Texas in April 2015

Taking it apart finally began in 2015. The contract was for about thirty-five million dollars.

  • USACE fact sheet (2015) and project page, 2015
    the STURGIS was towed 1,750 miles from Virginia to Galveston, Texas in April 2015

The bill passed fifty million.

  • CBS Texas, Sept. 17, 2018; Exchange Monitor, Sept. 28, 2018, 2018 partly confirmed
    increased the cost to $51 million

The last of the ship was scrapped in March 2019.

  • USACE fact sheet (2015) and project page, 2015
    the STURGIS was towed 1,750 miles from Virginia to Galveston, Texas in April 2015

So America's floating reactor made power for eight years. It took forty-three more before it was finally gone.

Our calculations

  • "Jogging speed" and "≈ 4.8 knots" (on screen): the tow from Murmansk (Aug. 23, 2019) to the mooring at Pevek (Sept. 14, 2019) took 22 days for about 4,700 km (Rosatom). 4,700 km ÷ 22 days ≈ 214 km a day ≈ 8.9 km/h ≈ 4.8 knots. A brisk jog is roughly 8 to 10 km/h; the comparison is ours.
  • "Roughly a third of its full electrical output": net electricity reported for the two units to the IAEA Power Reactor Information System (PRIS) for 2020-2025, divided by what they would have made running all year at full net electrical capacity (64 MWe for the plant). Average about 30% (about 39% in 2024, about 25% in 2025). Heat delivered to the town is not counted.
  • "Roughly four times that": 37.3 billion rubles (reported 2015 estimate) ÷ 9.1 billion rubles (2006 estimate) ≈ 4.1, in rubles of each year, not adjusted for inflation.
  • "Less than a quarter of that": discounted business rates of 7.7 to 9.0 rubles per kWh against a full-cost rate of more than 41.6 rubles per kWh (Chukotka tariff committee, 2025 rates): 9.0 ÷ 41.6 ≈ 0.22.
  • "About a seventh of the Russian plant's power": 10 MWe (Sturgis) ÷ 70 MW (Akademik Lomonosov, electrical) ≈ 1/7.
  • "Eight years": the Sturgis supplied power from 1968 to 1976. "Thirty-seven years": in storage from 1978 to the start of dismantling in 2015. "Forty-three more": from shutdown in 1976 to the end of scrapping in March 2019. "Five months later": March 2019 to Aug. 23, 2019.
  • "≈ 2.3 km" (on screen, St. Petersburg): Greenpeace's distance from the shipyard to the city center. Our check on OpenStreetMap, from the nearest edge of the Baltic Shipyard to St. Isaac's Cathedral: 2.31 km.
  • "70 MW ↔ ≈ 300 MW" (on screen): the Baimskaya mine's expected power need, about 300 MWe (World Nuclear News, 2022), against the Akademik Lomonosov's 70 MW.

Worth knowing

  • Rosatom has not published the final cost of the Akademik Lomonosov. The dollar range on screen (about $458 to $480 million) comes from outside estimates in 2018 and 2019 (Bellona; Bloomberg) at the exchange rates of the time; it is not an official figure.
  • "As far as we can find, it's the only one running today": based on a search of IAEA PRIS and Bellona's reporting, November 2026. Other floating units are planned or under construction; none of them was supplying a grid when we checked.
  • Rosatom's safety statements in the video (ten watertight compartments, staying afloat with two flooded, the breakwater) are the company's own claims; we found no independent check.
  • The tariff figures are rates for the whole Chukotka (Chaun-Bilibino) grid, not the cost of this plant's electricity alone, which is not published.
  • We could not confirm from a public source how the plant's construction cost is being recovered.
  • The Greenpeace quote is spelled "Chornobyl" as in Greenpeace's original 2018 text; the narrator uses the common English pronunciation.
  • Mine locations on the Chukotka map are schematic, except the Peschanka site of the Baimskaya project (OpenStreetMap). The Northern Sea Route line is schematic; it does not show the exact track of the tow.

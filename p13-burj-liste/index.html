The tower where it's still daytime

This is the Burj Khalifa, just after sunset.

  • Geonomics calculation (see Calculations below), 2026

At its base, the streetlights are already on. At the top, it's still daytime.

  • Geonomics calculation (see Calculations below), 2026

Near the top, the sun sets about four minutes late.

  • Geonomics calculation (see Calculations below), 2026

So how does the sun set twice on the Burj Khalifa? In theory, you could watch it set from the street, ride an elevator up for a minute, and watch it set again.

  • Geonomics calculation (see Calculations below), 2026
  • Gizmodo (2012), 2012 partly confirmed
  • Emaar Properties, Burj Khalifa fact sheet (Wayback Machine copy, Aug. 13, 2024), 2024

And this isn't just a tourist story. Dubai's fatwa center once told people on the upper floors to break their Ramadan fast later than their neighbors downstairs.

  • The National, Rym Ghazal (Aug. 8, 2011), reporting the ruling of the Islamic Affairs and Charitable Activities Department (IACAD), Dubai, 2011

  • Gulf News, via WAM (the UAE's state news agency), 'Burj Khalifa residents told to fast longer during Ramadan' (Aug. 2011), 2011
    This difference in timing on the 160-storey building is because sun sets later for residents on top floors

The reason isn't an illusion. From that high up, you can see farther around the curve of the Earth, so the sun stays in view a little longer.

1 of 7 · What is it standing on?

You'd expect the world's tallest building to stand on solid rock. Down here, there isn't any.

  • Badelow & Poulos 2016 (JGS), section 4.2 and Table 1, 2016
    Groundwater levels are generally high – approximately 2.5 m below ground level

Under the tower, it's loose sand, and then weak, crumbly sandstone.

  • Badelow & Poulos 2016 (JGS), section 4.2 and Table 1, 2016
    Groundwater levels are generally high – approximately 2.5 m below ground level

So the tower stands on nearly two hundred concrete piles.

  • Emaar Properties, Burj Khalifa fact sheet (Wayback Machine copy, Aug. 13, 2024), 2024

  • Badelow & Poulos (Coffey), 'Geotechnical foundation design for some of the world's tallest buildings', JGS Special Publication 2(2), 2016, 2016

Each one is a meter and a half across.

4 of 7 · Why does it change shape on the way up?

This stop is a swimming pool, more than two hundred seventy meters above the street.

  • Emaar Properties, Burj Khalifa fact sheet (Wayback Machine copy, Aug. 13, 2024), 2024
    World's highest swimming pool on Level 76 (over 270 metres; over 885 ft)

People live at this height. If you lived up here, you wouldn't want to feel the building move. So its designers worked to keep that motion small.

  • Emaar Properties, Burj Khalifa fact sheet (Wayback Machine copy, Aug. 13, 2024), 2024
    World's highest swimming pool on Level 76 (over 270 metres; over 885 ft)
  • Irwin & Baker 2008 (CTBUH), 2008
    Initial predictions obtained in May 2003, at over 37 milli-g for the 5 year return period

Wind doesn't just push on a tall building. As it flows around, it peels off in swirls, one side, then the other, in a steady rhythm.

  • Abdelrazaq, SMAR 2011 (Samsung C&T; at SOM during design), 2011
    to prevent lock-in vibration due to the vortex shedding

If that rhythm matches the tower's own, the whole thing starts to rock.

  • Abdelrazaq, SMAR 2011 (Samsung C&T; at SOM during design), 2011
    to prevent lock-in vibration due to the vortex shedding

So look at the shape. The tower has three wings, and every few floors, one of them steps back.

  • Peter Irwin (RWDI) and William Baker (SOM), 'The Wind Engineering of the Burj Dubai Tower', CTBUH 8th World Congress, 2008, 2008
    This process resulted in a substantial reduction in wind forces on the tower by “confusing” the wind
  • Abdelrazaq, SMAR 2011 (Samsung C&T; at SOM during design), 2011
    to prevent lock-in vibration due to the vortex shedding

That gives it a slightly different shape every few floors. Each shape sheds its swirls at a different rhythm, so they don't line up into one big push.

  • Abdelrazaq, SMAR 2011 (Samsung C&T; at SOM during design), 2011
    to prevent lock-in vibration due to the vortex shedding
  • Emaar Properties, Burj Khalifa fact sheet (Wayback Machine copy, Aug. 13, 2024), 2024
    preventing it from becoming ‘organised’ and therefore minimising lateral movement

The engineers had their own word for this. They were "confusing" the wind.

  • Peter Irwin (RWDI) and William Baker (SOM), 'The Wind Engineering of the Burj Dubai Tower', CTBUH 8th World Congress, 2008, 2008
    This process resulted in a substantial reduction in wind forces on the tower by “confusing” the wind

It took more than one try. After each round of wind-tunnel tests, they reshaped the tower and moved the steps.

  • Peter Irwin (RWDI) and William Baker (SOM), 'The Wind Engineering of the Burj Dubai Tower', CTBUH 8th World Congress, 2008, 2008
    This process resulted in a substantial reduction in wind forces on the tower by “confusing” the wind

They even turned the whole building, so the strong winds Dubai gets most often would tend to hit the tips of its wings.

  • Peter Irwin (RWDI) and William Baker (SOM), 'The Wind Engineering of the Burj Dubai Tower', CTBUH 8th World Congress, 2008, 2008
    This process resulted in a substantial reduction in wind forces on the tower by “confusing” the wind

Together with better wind data, those changes cut the predicted shaking at the top by about two-thirds, on paper.

  • Irwin & Baker 2008 (CTBUH), 2008
    Initial predictions obtained in May 2003, at over 37 milli-g for the 5 year return period

6 of 7 · Why does the sun set late up here?

Our calculations

  • "Near the top, the sun sets about four minutes late" / "more than three minutes" at the main deck / "four minutes or more" at the tip: we used the U.S. Naval Observatory definition of sunset (upper edge of the sun on a sea-level horizon, standard refraction) and asked how much further the sun must sink before it disappears for an observer at height h above the sea. The sun's last ray grazes the sea at the observer's horizon point, so the extra sinking is about the arc of the Earth to that point: 2.07 arc-minutes times the square root of h in meters (Bowditch, Appendix B: distance to the horizon D = 2.07 √h nautical miles; one nautical mile is about one arc-minute). We divided that angle by the rate at which the sun sinks at Dubai's latitude (25.2° N) on the day. Results: main deck, level 124 (452 m): 3.2-3.6 minutes; level 148 (555 m): 3.6-4.0; highest occupied floor (585 m): 3.7-4.1; tip (828 m): 4.4-4.9, depending on the season. Real refraction varies from day to day, which can move these figures by about a minute.
  • "The real gap comes out a little longer than the ruling": for mid-Ramadan 2011 (Aug. 15), the same method gives about 2.7 minutes at level 80 (about 287 m, our estimate from the floor heights) and about 3.8 minutes at level 150 (about 563 m, our estimate); the 2011 ruling added 2 and 3 minutes.
  • "Your horizon: about 80 km away" (on screen): distance to the sea horizon with standard refraction, 3.86 √h km, for h = 452 m: about 82 km (about 76 km without refraction). From the street (eye at about 1.7 m): about 5 km.
  • Light boundary (on screen, last scene): the lowest height that still sees the sun t minutes after street-level sunset, same method, equinox: about 390 m after 3 minutes, 530 m after 3.5 minutes, 690 m after 4 minutes.
  • "About a sixth of the water the tower uses": 15 million US gallons a year (design estimate) is about 41,000 gallons, or about 156,000 liters, a day; the tower uses about 946,000 liters a day (fact sheet); 156,000 / 946,000 ≈ 0.16.
  • "About twenty Olympic pools": 15 million US gallons ≈ 57 million liters; an Olympic pool holds at least 2.5 million liters; 57 / 2.5 ≈ 23, which the developer rounds to about 20.
  • "More than a third of a mile": about 600 m = 0.37 miles.
  • "About twenty-nine percent of the building's height": 244 m of 829.8 m = 29.4% (CTBUH, 2013).
  • "Cut the predicted shaking at the top by about two-thirds": predicted peak acceleration at the top floors for a five-year wind fell from more than 37 milli-g (May 2003) to about 12 milli-g, a drop of about 68%. About half of the first cut (37 to 19) came from better wind data; the rest from re-orienting and reshaping the tower and from aeroelastic model tests (Irwin & Baker 2008).
  • "Roughly four hundred meters up" (stop five): the window-washing machines' zone starts at level 109 (Kersten); about 396 m by our interpolation between level 76 (above 270 m) and level 124 (452 m), about 3.75 m per floor.
  • "Three weeks later": Dec. 14, 2009 to Jan. 4, 2010 = 21 days.
  • "About one point one billion dollars": AED 4.2 billion at the fixed rate of 3.6725 dirhams per dollar ≈ $1.14 billion (the press release gives US$1.1 billion).

Worth knowing

  • We could not find an audited construction cost. The figure in the video, about $1.5 billion, is what the developer, Emaar, told the Associated Press at the opening.
  • We found no official statement that links the tower's new name to Abu Dhabi's December 2009 support. Officially, the name honored Sheikh Khalifa bin Zayed Al Nahyan, then ruler of Abu Dhabi and president of the UAE (he died in 2022). Emaar says the opening date marked the fourth anniversary of Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid's accession as ruler of Dubai.
  • We could not find a solid first-hand account of anyone watching the sun set at street level and again from the tower on the same evening. The story is widely repeated in popular press (Gizmodo, 2012).
  • Emaar does not publish separate revenue for the tower or its observation decks; the $1.1 billion figure is its whole hospitality, leisure and entertainment business, hotels included.
  • The 2011 ruling (levels 80 and 150) was replaced in 2019 by an IACAD circular with cutoffs at levels 60 and 121.
  • Floor heights in meters marked "our estimate" or "our interpolation" are not published; we interpolated between published levels.

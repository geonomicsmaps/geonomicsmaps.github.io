Why Hasn't the Leaning Tower of Pisa Fallen Over?

Geonomics. Every narrated claim with its source and a short quote in the original language. Errors? Say so in the video's comments; corrections are pinned.

It almost fell

In September 1995, in the middle of the night, the Leaning Tower of Pisa suddenly began leaning faster.

  • PBS NOVA, 1995 partly confirmed

In about two days, it moved further than it used to move in a whole year.

  • A7 ve A10'dan hesap, 1995

So why hasn't the Leaning Tower of Pisa fallen over? The answer starts with the soft ground under it. And it ends with an eleven-year rescue that moved the tower so little you'd never notice from the square.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1990
    half a degree which is not enough to be visible
  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    re-opened in December 2001

Most people think engineers fixed the lean. They corrected only about a tenth of it, and on purpose.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 2002
    about 10% of the maximum value reached in 1993
  • Building (2000), John Burland, 2000
    There's no way we could have had a not leaning tower

What is a 14,500-ton tower standing on?

Pisa stands on a flat plain built by rivers and the sea.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    soft estuarine deposits of sandy and clayey silts laid down under tidal conditions

Under the tower, the first ten meters or so are silt and sand, laid down by tides. Below that, soft marine clay runs down to about forty meters.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    soft estuarine deposits of sandy and clayey silts laid down under tidal conditions

Now put about fourteen and a half thousand metric tons of stone on top.

  • Burland (2001), Ingenia, "The stabilisation of the Leaning Tower of Pisa"; Wikipedia

Over the centuries, the tower sank into this ground by about three meters on average.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    average settlements of between 3.0m and 3.5m

Here's the trap. The ground doesn't have to break. It just has to be too soft.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    not due to lack of strength of the ground but is due to insufficient stiffness

Picture a pencil standing on a mattress. Nudge it, and it tips a little, and its own weight keeps it going. Engineers call that leaning instability.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    not due to lack of strength of the ground but is due to insufficient stiffness

By the 1990s, the soil under the tower's south edge was starting to yield.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1993
    the soil in a state of local yield

And if you look around the city, you'll find it isn't the only old bell tower here that leans. At least two others in Pisa do too.

  • In Your Pocket; Youth Time partly confirmed

The whole plain is soft, and it is still very slowly sinking.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 2009
    the general subsidence of the Pisa plain

It started leaning before it was finished

The tower took nearly two centuries to build, and the waiting is what mattered. Construction started in 1173. After about five years, work stopped, for nearly a century.

  • Burland (2001), Ingenia, "The stabilisation of the Leaning Tower of Pisa"
    Work recommenced nearly 100 years later in about 1272
  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1173
    construction began in 1173 and continued (with two long interruptions)

The usual explanation is war: Pisa was fighting its neighbors. But the records are thin.

  • Wikipedia; New World Encyclopedia partly confirmed

When building restarted, around 1272, the tower began to tilt south. Then work stopped again, for another eighty years or so.

  • Burland (2001), Ingenia, "The stabilisation of the Leaning Tower of Pisa"
    Work recommenced nearly 100 years later in about 1272
  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    commenced leaning southwards during the second construction stage

The engineers who would later rescue it concluded that those long pauses did the saving first. The clay had time to settle and get stronger. Built in one go, it would have fallen over.

  • Burland (2001), Ingenia, "The stabilisation of the Leaning Tower of Pisa"; Building (2000)
    had the Tower been completed at this stage, it would have fallen over

When the masons came back, they set wedge-shaped courses of stone at each floor to bend the tower back away from the lean.

  • Burland (2001), Ingenia, "The stabilisation of the Leaning Tower of Pisa"
    tapered blocks of masonry were placed at the level of each floor

Why did touching it make things worse?

In 1838, the architect Alessandro Gherardesca had the ground around the base dug out, to expose the tower's buried steps and column bases. On the south side, water flooded in. The lean got worse.

  • PBS NOVA, citing Ingenia, 1838 partly confirmed

In 1934, engineers drilled hundreds of holes into the foundation and pumped in grout to strengthen it. The tower jumped south, by about a centimeter.

  • PBS NOVA, 1934
    2026-11-08
  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    apart from two occasions in 1934 and the early 1970s when man had intervened

Even the weather pushes it. If you stood next to it on a stormy night in October, you'd never feel it. Normally, the groundwater on the south side sits a little higher than on the north, and that actually helps hold the tower up. But every autumn, heavy rain shrinks that difference, and the tower rotates a tiny bit further south.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1995
    a fluctuating ground-water level due to seasonal heavy rainstorms
  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 2002
    a small, but not negligible stabilising moment

By 1990, the top of the tower was moving about a millimeter and a half a year, twice as fast as in the 1930s. It sounds like nothing. But the stone was overstressed, and the soil under the south edge was close to its limit.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1990
    equivalent to a horizontal movement at the top of about 1.5mm per year
  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1990
    the risk of sudden structural collapse
  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1993
    the soil in a state of local yield

Then, in March 1989, a medieval bell tower in Pavia, in northern Italy, collapsed without warning. Four people died.

  • Wikipedia (Civic Tower (Pavia); Leaning Tower of Pisa), 1989 partly confirmed

Ten months later, Italy closed the Leaning Tower of Pisa.

  • Wikipedia (Civic Tower (Pavia); Leaning Tower of Pisa), 1989 partly confirmed
  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    re-opened in December 2001

An international committee of engineers was set up to save it, chaired by the Italian engineer Michele Jamiolkowski. One of its members was the British engineer John Burland.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1990
    the risk of sudden structural collapse
  • arama özetleri; Building (2000), 1990 partly confirmed

Later, they described the tower in those years as being on the point of falling over.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1990
    a Tower that was on the point of falling over

Which side was actually sinking?

First, they wrapped the lower floors in steel bands, so the overstressed marble wouldn't give way.

  • Burland (2001), Ingenia, "The stabilisation of the Leaning Tower of Pisa"; NOVA, 1992

Then they looked closely at about eighty years of measurements, and found something nobody expected.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    a most surprising form of motion

For decades, most experts had assumed the south side was simply sinking. If you'd looked at the tower, you'd have thought so too. The data said otherwise. The middle of the base wasn't moving up or down at all. The whole tower was pivoting like a seesaw: south edge down, north edge up.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    a most surprising form of motion

That gave them an idea. If the north side was lifting, weigh it down. So they stacked lead on the north side: six hundred metric tons of it.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    a most surprising form of motion
  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1993
    600t of lead weights on the north side

And it worked. The tower crept back a little.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1993
    600t of lead weights on the north side

Black September

Which brings us back to that night in September 1995. It started with a reasonable idea. You can't leave a pile of lead next to a world monument forever. The plan was to replace it with anchors hidden in the ground. To protect the digging, workers froze the wet ground with liquid nitrogen.

  • PBS NOVA, 1995 partly confirmed

When the freezing stopped, the tower began moving south. In the middle of the night. About a millimeter a day at the top, and speeding up.

  • PBS NOVA, 1995 partly confirmed
  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1995
    has been called “Black September”

The anchor plan was dropped, and more lead went on, until nine hundred metric tons were holding the tower back.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1995
    has been called “Black September”

The engineers would later call it "Black September".

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1995
    has been called “Black September”

How do you dig under a falling tower?

The idea wasn't new. An Italian engineer had proposed it for Pisa back in 1962. And it had been used in Mexico City, to stop the great cathedral there from sinking unevenly.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    notably to reduce the damaging differential settlements within the Metropolitan Cathedral of Mexico City

It's called underexcavation. You don't push the tower. You take a little soil out from under the high side, the north. The ground closes into the gap, that side sinks a fraction, and the tower rolls back toward upright. The danger was the south side. In the engineers' own words, any slight disturbance to the ground there "would almost certainly trigger collapse".

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1990
    a Tower that was on the point of falling over

So before touching the tower, they practiced. They built a big test foundation and drilled under that first. The drill spins inside a protective tube, so it barely disturbs the ground.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1990
    The trial foundation was successfully rotated by about 0.25o

It worked: they tilted the test foundation by about a quarter of a degree.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1990
    The trial foundation was successfully rotated by about 0.25o

Then, once, they took soil from the wrong side of a line. The test foundation turned the wrong way. It took six weeks to steady it again.

  • Burland (2001), Ingenia, "The stabilisation of the Leaning Tower of Pisa", 1990
    It took six weeks to regain stability of the trial foundation

Better to learn that on a fake tower. They also set up a routine: every day, a report on how the ground had moved. Then a signed order for the next day's digging.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1990
    The trial foundation was successfully rotated by about 0.25o

In December 1998, they added safety cables. They were tied high on the tower and stretched far to the north, ready to hold it if it moved the wrong way.

  • Burland (2001), Ingenia, "The stabilisation of the Leaning Tower of Pisa"; NOVA, 1992

February the ninth, 1999. The engineers later called the mood "great tension".

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1999
    in an atmosphere of great tension, the first soil extraction took place

Twelve holes. The first soil came out. And the tower began to turn north. In 2000, they put in forty-one holes along the whole north side, and took the soil out a little at a time.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 2000-2001
    the last lead ingot was removed
  • Building (2000), 2000 partly confirmed
    about 100 litres a day

On the sixteenth of January 2001, they lifted off the last lead ingot.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 2000-2001
    the last lead ingot was removed

And in 2002, they finally tamed the autumn rain: three wells on the north side now keep the water there low and steady.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 2002
    a small, but not negligible stabilising moment

Why stop at half a degree?

So how much did they straighten it? Half a degree.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1990
    half a degree which is not enough to be visible
  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 2002
    about 10% of the maximum value reached in 1993

About a tenth of its tilt.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 2002
    about 10% of the maximum value reached in 1993

Before the rescue, the south edge of the base was pressing into the ground about twice as hard as the average. The north edge was barely touching it.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1993
    the soil in a state of local yield

So the target was only half a degree. Enough to take pressure off the stone and the soil on the south side. Not enough for a casual visitor to notice.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1990
    half a degree which is not enough to be visible

Burland put it simply: "There's no way we could have had a not leaning tower."

  • Building (2000), John Burland, 2000
    There's no way we could have had a not leaning tower

Will it ever fall?

The tower reopened in December 2001.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    re-opened in December 2001

If you stand under it today, it leans about as much as it did in the early 1800s.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 2009
    at least 200 years

By 2018, the engineers said it had straightened by about four more centimeters on its own.

  • ANSA (committee, quoting Squeglia), 2018 partly confirmed
    Tower of Pisa has lost 4 cm of its lean

Even in the pessimistic case they considered, it would take at least two hundred years to lean as far as it did in 1993.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 2009
    at least 200 years

Nearly two centuries to build, most of it waiting. About seven and a half centuries of leaning. Eleven years to rescue it, on a budget of about twenty-five million pounds from the Italian government.

  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3), 1173
    construction began in 1173 and continued (with two long interruptions)
  • Burland (2001), Ingenia, "The stabilisation of the Leaning Tower of Pisa"
    Work recommenced nearly 100 years later in about 1272
  • Burland, Jamiolkowski, Viggiani (2009), IJGCH 1(3)
    re-opened in December 2001
  • Institution of Civil Engineers (ICE), 1990-2001 partly confirmed

In September 1995, it was moving a millimeter a day. Today, it's stable.

  • PBS NOVA, 1995 partly confirmed

Our calculations

  • Tower weight: 14,500 metric tons (Burland et al. 2009) × 1.1023 ≈ 16,000 short tons (our conversion).
  • Black September speed: about 4 arc seconds a day (NOVA). Burland et al. give 6 arc seconds a year ≈ 1.5 mm a year at the top in 1990, so 1 arc second ≈ 0.25 mm at the top and 4 arc seconds a day ≈ 1 mm a day (our calculation).
  • Two days at that speed (≈ 8 arc seconds) is more than the tower's whole 1990 yearly movement (≈ 6 arc seconds); hence "about" in the narration (our calculation).
  • "Half a degree": the foundation tilt fell by 1,880 arc seconds by June 2002; 1,880 ÷ 3,600 ≈ 0.52° (Burland et al. 2009 give this as about 10% of the 1993 maximum).
  • "About seven and a half centuries of leaning": the southward lean began in the 1270s (Burland 2001); 2026 − 1272 ≈ 750 years. "Nearly two centuries to build": 1173 to about 1370.

Worth knowing

  • The night-time start of Black September and the 4 arc seconds a day come from one secondary source (PBS NOVA). The failed ground-anchor attempt, the southward acceleration and the 900 metric tons of lead are confirmed by Burland, Jamiolkowski and Viggiani (2009).
  • About £25 million is the reported budget for the rescue (Institution of Civil Engineers), not an audited final cost.
  • The safety cables of December 1998 were a temporary precaution, ready to hold the tower if it moved the wrong way. They did not pull it upright; soil extraction did.
  • "At least two hundred years" is the engineers' pessimistic case for the tower returning to its 1993 lean. It is not a prediction that it will fall.
  • The extra straightening of about 4 cm since 2001 was reported in 2018 by the monitoring committee, through the Italian news agency ANSA.
  • The Pavia tower collapse date and toll come from secondary sources; the 1990 closure of the Pisa tower is confirmed by Burland et al. (2009).

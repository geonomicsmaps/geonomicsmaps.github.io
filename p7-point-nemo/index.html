It's Russia's Mir, brought down on purpose.

In a few years, NASA plans to send something far bigger after it: the International Space Station. Why will NASA sink it near a place called Point Nemo?

You might think old spacecraft just burn up on the way down. The big ones don't.

  • ESA, Space Debris FAQ (Nis 2021), 2021

And this is the biggest thing ever built in space.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    the single largest structure ever built in space

It's falling right now

Start with the size. The station is more than four hundred and thirty metric tons.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    operates in LEO around 257 miles (415 km) in altitude and as a mass of more than 945,000lbs (430,000kg)

And here's the strange part: it's falling right now. Up where it flies, there's still a thin trace of air.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    operates in LEO around 257 miles (415 km) in altitude and as a mass of more than 945,000lbs (430,000kg)
  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    orbital lifetime ... at this altitude is roughly one-to-two years without reboosts

It drags on the station and pulls it a little lower every day. So it has to be pushed back up, again and again.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    orbital lifetime ... at this altitude is roughly one-to-two years without reboosts

Stop pushing, and within one to two years, it comes down on its own.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    orbital lifetime ... at this altitude is roughly one-to-two years without reboosts

It's also getting old. Its first modules were designed for thirty years in orbit.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    originally designed for a 30-year structural life in LEO. In 2030, the FGB and Node 1 will have been operating for 32 years

By the end of this decade, they'll be past that.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    originally designed for a 30-year structural life in LEO. In 2030, the FGB and Node 1 will have been operating for 32 years

Every other way out was worse

NASA went through the alternatives.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    NASA evaluated several alternative options to deorbit, including: Uncontrolled re-entry; Disassembly and return to Earth; ... Boosting to a higher orbit; Transitioning the space station to a commercial

Bring it home? It was never designed to be easily taken apart in space, and nothing flying today could carry the big pieces down.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    to be easily disassembled in space ... would require ... EVAs by space station crew, extensive planning

Reuse it, or hand it over to a company? NASA says no company came back with a workable proposal.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    NASA received no feasible proposals to utilize the hardware beyond the life of the space station

So why not push it higher, where the air barely drags on it? You'd think that's the safe option. It's the opposite.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    the mean time between an impact event decreases from ~51 years at the current operational altitude to less than four years at a 497 mile (800km), ~700-year orbit …

Higher up, there's far more space junk. Where the station flies now, NASA expects a hit big enough to break it apart about once every fifty years.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    the mean time between an impact event decreases from ~51 years at the current operational altitude to less than four years at a 497 mile (800km), ~700-year orbit …

Now move it up to eight hundred kilometers. There, it could happen in under four years.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    the mean time between an impact event decreases from ~51 years at the current operational altitude to less than four years at a 497 mile (800km), ~700-year orbit …

And a shattered station up there wouldn't come down for centuries. NASA warns it could limit, or even cut off, access to low orbit for that long.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    the mean time between an impact event decreases from ~51 years at the current operational altitude to less than four years at a 497 mile (800km), ~700-year orbit …

In low orbit, there's no safe "up." The only way out is down.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    the mean time between an impact event decreases from ~51 years at the current operational altitude to less than four years at a 497 mile (800km), ~700-year orbit …
  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    command a large re-entry burn, providing the final push ... Once the debris enters the ocean, it is expected to settle to the ocean floor

Why it won't just burn up

Engineers call the trip back down reentry. A spacecraft comes back so fast that the air in front of it can't get out of the way. It gets squeezed, and it turns white-hot.

  • ESA, Space Debris FAQ
    air drag converts orbital energy into heat

From a beach below, you'd see a fireball breaking into smaller fireballs. Most of the spacecraft melts and burns. But not all of it.

  • ESA, Space Debris FAQ (Nis 2021), 2021
    Approximately 20-40% of the mass of larger-size spacecraft or rocket bodies, or parts made of particularly high-melting steel or titanium alloys, may survive the reentry

For big spacecraft, the European Space Agency estimates that twenty to forty percent of the mass can survive, mostly steel and titanium.

  • ESA, Space Debris FAQ (Nis 2021), 2021
    Approximately 20-40% of the mass of larger-size spacecraft or rocket bodies, or parts made of particularly high-melting steel or titanium alloys, may survive the reentry

Now picture the space station doing that.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    operates in LEO around 257 miles (415 km) in altitude and as a mass of more than 945,000lbs (430,000kg)

Its modules, its giant frame, its tanks. NASA's own description of an uncontrolled fall is blunt: "very large pieces of debris with a large debris footprint."

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    re-entering spacecraft must meet or exceed a 1-in-10,000 likelihood of public risk ... uncontrolled re-entry would result in very large pieces of debris with a large debris footprint

And there's a rule. For U.S. government spacecraft, the chance that a reentry hurts anyone should stay below one in ten thousand.

  • US Government Orbital Debris Mitigation Standard Practices (2019), 2019
    The risk of human casualty from surviving components with impact kinetic energies greater than 15 joules should be less than 0.0001 (1 in 10,000)
  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    re-entering spacecraft must meet or exceed a 1-in-10,000 likelihood of public risk ... uncontrolled re-entry would result in very large pieces of debris with a large debris footprint

Falling wherever it happens to fall, the space station can't meet that.

  • NASA (2024), International Space Station Deorbit Analysis Summary, 2024
    re-entering spacecraft must meet or exceed a 1-in-10,000 likelihood of public risk ... uncontrolled re-entry would result in very large pieces of debris with a large debris footprint

At about seventy-seven metric tons, it was the heaviest spacecraft ever to fall back to Earth up to that time.

  • NASA History, 45 Years Ago: Skylab Reenters (11 Tem 2024), 2024

  • NASA History, 45 Years Ago: Skylab Reenters (11 Tem 2024), 2024

The debris trail stretched for thousands of kilometers, across the Indian Ocean and into Western Australia.

  • NASA History, 45 Years Ago: Skylab Reenters (11 Tem 2024), 2024

People in a few Outback towns heard sonic booms.

The loneliest spot on Earth

So where do you aim something that big? As far away from people as you can get. But where exactly is that? If you like stories where the answer is hiding on a map, subscribe. Here comes one. In 1992, a survey engineer named Hrvoje Lukatela went looking for that exact place.

  • Guinness World Records, Remotest spot from land
    first identified in 1992 by Croatian-Canadian survey engineer Hrvoje Lukatela ... using a geospatial computer programme

He didn't need a ship. He used a computer program and the coastlines of the whole planet.

  • Guinness World Records, Remotest spot from land
    first identified in 1992 by Croatian-Canadian survey engineer Hrvoje Lukatela ... using a geospatial computer programme

His question was simple: which point in the ocean is farthest from any land? The answer is in the South Pacific. It sits almost the same distance from three tiny specks of land: Ducie Island, Motu Nui near Easter Island, and Maher Island off Antarctica.

  • NOAA National Ocean Service, Where is Point Nemo? (4 Oca 2021), 2021
    located at coordinates 48°52.6′S 123°23.6′W ... about 2,688 kilometers from the nearest land—Ducie Island, part of the Pitcairn Islands
  • NOAA, Where is Point Nemo? (4 Oca 2021)
    Ducie Island (north), Motu Nui (northeast), Maher Island (south, Antarctica)

Each is about two thousand seven hundred kilometers away.

  • NOAA National Ocean Service, Where is Point Nemo? (4 Oca 2021), 2021
    located at coordinates 48°52.6′S 123°23.6′W ... about 2,688 kilometers from the nearest land—Ducie Island, part of the Pitcairn Islands

It's named after Captain Nemo, from Jules Verne. In Latin, nemo means "no one."

  • NOAA, Where is Point Nemo?
    popularly known as 'Point Nemo,' after the submarine captain in Jules Verne's novel

And here's what makes it so strange. When the space station passes overhead, the closest humans to Point Nemo usually aren't on a ship or an island. They're the astronauts, about four hundred kilometers straight up.

It's already a graveyard

The space station won't be the first thing sent to these waters. Not even close. By one count, more than two hundred and sixty spacecraft were brought down in this part of the ocean.

  • Wikipedia Spacecraft cemetery; IFLScience; Ripley's, 2016 partly confirmed
    dumped some 263 pieces of space debris since 1971

Most were Soviet and Russian: cargo ships, and several old space stations.

  • Wikipedia Spacecraft cemetery; IFLScience; Ripley's, 2016 partly confirmed
    dumped some 263 pieces of space debris since 1971

Europe's first cargo ship for the space station was named Jules Verne, after the same writer. Fittingly, it ended up here too.

  • ESA press release 41/2008, 2008
    controlled destructive re-entry over a completely uninhabited area of the South Pacific

And the new spacecraft fires one final, powerful burn, pushing the station steeply into the atmosphere.

The angle matters. A shallow, lazy fall smears debris across a huge stretch of the planet. A steep plunge keeps it inside a smaller, more predictable area, one you can actually aim.

Our calculations

  • Station mass: more than 430,000 kg (NASA 2024) ≈ 474 short tons (× 1.1023, our conversion); narration says "more than four hundred and thirty metric tons".
  • "More than five times heavier than Skylab": 430 ÷ 76.7 ≈ 5.6 (NASA 2024; NASA History 2024, 169,000 lb ≈ 76,700 kg).
  • Skylab debris footprint: 2,450 miles (NASA) ≈ 3,943 km (× 1.609).
  • Point Nemo to Ducie Island, Motu Nui and Maher Island: about 2,688 km each (NOAA); our great-circle check from the listed coordinates gives 2,683-2,695 km.
  • Mir's planned aim point (about 47°S 140°W, ESA) is about 1,250 km west of Point Nemo (48°52.6′S 123°23.6′W, NOAA), great-circle distance, our calculation.
  • "Two years before the first piece of the station flew": the environmental review is dated May 1996 (NASA, NTRS 19960053133); the first modules launched in 1998.
  • Station ground track on the maps: a 51.6° orbit (NASA 2024), Earth turning about 22.9° per orbit, our calculation; illustrative, not a real pass.

Worth knowing

  • "More than 260 spacecraft" is one count (1971-2016) from secondary sources; there is no official public tally.
  • The shaded area around Point Nemo is approximate: NASA calls the target "the South Pacific Oceanic Uninhabited Area (SPOUA), the area around Point Nemo" but we found no published boundary.
  • Mir's debris area on the map is ESA's planned area (3,000-4,000 km long, about 200 km wide); the actual spread was reported only in secondary sources.
  • The deorbit schedule and the end date can still change; NASA's 2024 summary and GAO-26-107805 describe the current plan, and Congress wants the station operating through at least September 2030.
